Pain, Ownership and Evaluation

Victor Wyld, Colin Klein and David Bain under Inference to the Best Explanation

Victor Wyld · November 4, 2026

Does identifying hurting with the physically constituted sensory occurrence explain anything that the strongest physicalist theories of pain do not? The comparison here places Victor Wyld's The Ownership Answer: What It Is Like alongside Colin Klein's imperativism and David Bain's evaluativism. It distinguishes a theory of painful sensation from a broader account of consciousness and the Hard Problem.

The provisional conclusion: Wyld's integration of sensory identity, objective Ownership and scrutinized awareness offers a distinct philosophical architecture. Ontological economy alone cannot establish its superiority to Klein or Bain, because their accounts can also be interpreted within an ordinary physical ontology. The remaining issue is explanatory reach and empirical discrimination, not a count of supposedly immaterial properties.

Three serious physicalist accounts

Wyld: sensory identity and objective Ownership

Wyld proposes an a posteriori identity: the hurting is the relevant physically constituted sensory occurrence, not a separate phenomenal property produced by that occurrence. Objective Ownership identifies whose occurrence it is by the pre-conscious relation of implication; it neither produces sensation nor creates a conscious viewpoint. Scrutinized awareness concerns the conscious threshold, not the manufacture of hurting. An occurrence can be objectively owned without consciousness, and a conscious episode need not yield a fresh acquisition or permanent memory.

These distinctions form part of a larger response to Chalmers. The negative tier challenges the inference from epistemic or modal separability to additional phenomenal ontology; the positive tier offers sensory identity and a participant-relative account of consciousness. Its scope is therefore wider than a local account of pain.

Klein: bodily imperatives

In What the Body Commands (2015), Colin Klein proposes that pain has imperative content: it directs the body to protect an injured part. This need not be an explicit linguistic thought or a consciously interpreted command. Klein distinguishes pain from suffering and explicitly addresses asymbolia and masochism. In his account, an asymbolic patient may retain the imperative while no longer treating the body as an authority to be obeyed. That is a substantive explanation, not a failure to understand how physical injury affects a conceptual instruction.

Bain: evaluative content

David Bain's evaluativism proposes that the unpleasantness of pain involves evaluative representational content—roughly, presenting a bodily condition as bad for the subject—rather than being reducible to an urge to withdraw. In Pains that Don't Hurt, Bain argues that pain asymbolia involves an abnormal experience in which an evaluative component is missing, in a subject also showing a deficit of bodily concern. The evaluative relation need not be a separate substance, a language-like judgment, or an extra fundamental law.

Pain asymbolia: a disputed test case

Some patients classified as having pain asymbolia identify painful stimulation yet show unusual indifference or reduced defensive response. The clinical and philosophical interpretation of their experience is contested. Griffith and Kind (2024) question whether the standard report warrants saying that pain itself persists; Duval and Klein (2026) defend a qualified version of the standard interpretation while emphasizing the diagnostic and taxonomic dispute. A published theory should not treat the patients' first-person reports as already settling the metaphysics.

Wyld's interpretation: sensory character may persist despite altered evaluation or motivation. Klein's interpretation: an imperative may continue to be issued even where the patient no longer accepts the body's authority. Bain's interpretation: the sensory representation and its evaluative unpleasantness may come apart. All three have resources to address dissociation. None obtains an automatic victory just by accommodating the case.

Nor should the lesion be described, without further evidence, as simply severing a route to scrutiny or carrying. An asymbolic patient might consciously recognize painful stimulation. SAT must then distinguish that scrutiny from the loss or alteration of distress, concern and motivation.

Benign masochism and the evaluation of sensation

Enjoyment of hot chili, demanding exercise or other normally aversive sensations puts pressure on any theory equating hurting with avoidance. But the experience may involve several changing components: sensory intensity, unpleasantness, context, expectation and higher-level evaluation. Klein can distinguish the issued protective imperative from an overriding or transformed response; Bain can distinguish sensory registration from evaluative unpleasantness; Wyld can distinguish the sensory occurrence from its motivational uptake. Which is best supported depends on fine-grained evidence about what changes in the experience, not a simplistic report that the person either likes or dislikes it.

What distinctive explanatory work does Ownership perform?

Ownership is objective implication: an occurrence belongs to whatever is implicated in it. A sheet of paper owns its tearing without sensation or an inner perspective. In a biological participant, Ownership likewise does not generate sensory character or perform the causal processing that produces a withdrawal response. Its proposed contribution is to mark the difference between an occurrence as undergone by its participant and that same occurrence as described from outside.

The question is whether that participant-relative distinction adds explanatory work beyond ordinary causal involvement, embodiment and bodily self-relation. Wyld offers it as part of a diagnosis of the explanatory gap: a description of an occurrence is not the having of it, and the epistemic difference does not by itself entail a second phenomenal object. But simply naming the Ownership relation does not prove physical identity. The identity thesis must retain independent abductive support, and the relevance of Ownership must be assessed rather than assumed.

Neither Klein nor Bain is thereby refuted. Their theories address the contents and motivational or evaluative structure of pain; Wyld's theory additionally proposes an account of the conscious threshold and the metaphysical demand generated by Chalmers's argument. That broader scope is a potential advantage only if its distinctions prove coherent, necessary and explanatorily useful.

A fair comparison under IBE

Inference to the Best Explanation is a truth-directed but fallible assessment. The relevant criteria include explanatory scope, empirical fit, causal integration, ontological economy, the independence of auxiliary assumptions and remaining difficulties. Wyld's proposal is attractive because it treats hurting as causally efficacious sensing and avoids positing an additional phenomenal ingredient. Yet Klein and Bain can share the same basic physical ontology; neither must be charged with creating new immaterial entities merely because it uses representational content.

What favours Wyld: a unified treatment of sensory identity, objective implication and scrutinized consciousness that explicitly engages the Hard Problem, rather than only pain's unpleasantness or motivational significance. What remains to be shown: that the relevant physical sensory occurrence can be specified without moving its boundaries after a counterexample; that scrutiny can be empirically distinguished from mere registration; and that the Ownership relation explains something not already explained by stronger causal or embodied accounts.

The qualified verdict: this comparison clarifies the potential reach of Wyld's two-tier account and why it merits consideration under IBE. It does not show that Klein and Bain are inferior merely because their theories invoke content, nor does pain asymbolia independently confirm Wyld's identity thesis. The broader philosophical argument for a solution to Chalmers is presented in the Ownership Answer overview and complete original paper.
