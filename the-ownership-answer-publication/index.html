Abstract

David Chalmers asks why the performance of physical functions is accompanied by experience. This paper answers: Ownership. Its argument has two tiers. The first holds that he argues for conceptual separability and draws from it more than it supports. His evidence shows that experience and physical process go together. That new fundamental laws, or a further phenomenal nature underlying the physical, are needed rests on further steps: the 1995 paper asserts or assumes them, and his developed argument rests them on two premises this paper contests. Ownership is the lasting relation by which an occurrence belongs to whatever is implicated in it. It holds before consciousness, it is not a function, and it accounts for the difference between having an occurrence and observing it. The second tier is a positive account drawn from four earlier papers, and it works at two levels. At the level of sensation, a pain is an occurrence of hurting, and the hurting is the sensory occurrence itself. That identity is offered as the best explanation of what is observed: hurting depends on specific mechanisms, goes where the sensory occurrence goes rather than where the damage is, does the work pain does, and has the character selection would favour. Asked what it is like to hurt, the only answer is that it is like hurting; nothing further accompanies it. At the level of consciousness, which the earlier papers define as scrutinized awareness, a conscious experience is awareness of what must be carried. Chalmers is right that something is left over when the functions are explained, but it is two things taken as one: the character of the sensing, which belongs to the occurrence, and what the participant carries, which needs no new law of nature. His error, on this account, is one of category: he treats what an experience is like for the one who has it as a further, irreducible property of the experience, one that needs its own law. If both tiers hold, the Hard Problem keeps its observation and loses its conclusion.

1. The question and the answer

David Chalmers states the Hard Problem in one sentence: "Why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience?" (Chalmers 1995, Section 3).

This paper begins with a prior question.

Why assume physical processes are accompanied by experience, rather than questioning whether this supposed accompaniment is an independently established relation in the first place?

Chalmers constructs his Hard Problem by first assuming a separability that he has not established.

"Accompanied" relates one thing to another. Chalmers does not hold that there are two events. He can grant a single occurrence with a physical aspect and a phenomenal aspect (his Section 7). What his question takes for granted is that the phenomenal aspect is something further, still to be explained once the physical account is complete. What is observed is one thing happening to one person.

His ground for treating it as further is an argument. He holds it conceptually coherent that the physical process should occur without experience (his Section 5). No actual case shows this. Ordinary processing goes on without experience, but a complete duplicate of a conscious occurrence with the experience removed has only been conceived. He agrees that the two never come apart in our world (his Section 7).

The answer proposed here is one word: Ownership. The proposal is that the process and the experience go together because they are one occurrence, and that it belongs to the participant implicated in it.

The paper argues for four things and then advances a thesis.

1. His evidence shows that conscious experience and certain physical processes go together. His conclusion, that fundamental laws must be added, needs two further steps: that what is conceivable is possible, and that the physical account is the base to which experience must be joined. The 1995 paper asserts the first and assumes the second. 2. Ownership is the objective relation by which an occurrence belongs to its participant. It is not a function, it holds before consciousness, and it needs no new law of nature. 3. Ownership puts the participant, and the difference between having an occurrence and observing it, in place before consciousness. Neither has to be explained by consciousness. 4. If the hurting is a further property, and the physical cause of waking is complete, and no one is woken twice over, then the hurting does not do the waking, unless, as on his later intrinsic-nature view, it is the nature of the physical cause itself (Section 6).

The thesis is that a conscious experience is awareness under scrutiny, exposed to what the participant already carries or newly acquires. A conscious sensation is one case of it. Something is left over, as Chalmers rightly says, and it is what the participant carries. It is not a further fact about how the experience feels. The paper says what supports the thesis and what would be needed to establish it.

The argument therefore has two tiers, and neither borrows certainty from the other. The first challenges whether his additional fundamental laws are required. The second is the thesis, which is

provisional. The dissolution offered is of a particular kind: his observation is kept, and his conclusion is removed.

Nothing real is denied in what follows. Pain is real, sensation is real, and consciousness is real.

Parts of this are not new. That the Hard Problem assumes a separation it has not earned has been argued before, and the first half of the thesis is close to a position long defended by David Rosenthal (Section 13). What this paper adds is the route and the second half: an audit of where the assumption enters Chalmers's text and argument; Ownership, a relation that holds before consciousness and is not a function; and the claim that what is left over is the remainder a participant carries.

Section 12 sets out his questions one by one and answers each.

This is the fifth paper in a series. The first proposed a threshold: awareness becomes consciousness under scrutiny (Wyld 2026a). The second examined how such distinctions are flattened once they circulate, with the Hard Problem as its case (Wyld 2026c). The third asked what becomes of a participant who carries what has happened to them (Wyld 2026b). The fourth asked how an implication first becomes the participant's own (Wyld 2026d).

Those papers did not set the Hard Problem aside. The first said that it "identifies the right territory" and that scrutinized awareness "proposes an answer within it." The second said that scrutinized awareness "does not ask the hard problem to disappear," and changes what it must answer. The fourth said that its own question was a different one. This paper goes further. It asks whether the demand for new fundamental laws survives, and in that respect it departs from the second paper.

2. Terms

Five terms carry the argument, and each is used in its plain sense.

  • Participant. Whoever or whatever is implicated in an occurrence. This paper says "participant" where Chalmers would say "subject," because he reserves "subject" for the subject of experience. The sense is broader than participanthood in the earlier papers, which is continuation under carried implication (Wyld 2026b).
  • Ownership. An occurrence belongs to whatever is objectively implicated in it, from the moment it occurs. The moment of objective implication is the moment of Ownership. Ownership does not wait for awareness, recognition or memory. It is also lasting, which does not mean everlasting. One occurrence can have several participants, and each owns it differently: a surgeon owns an amputation as the one who performs it, the patient as the one it is done to.
  • Undergoing. Having something happen to you. "Undergo" suggests that the event happens and is then over. Ownership means that it lasts. A sheet of paper undergoes being torn, and it owns being torn: from then on it is a sheet that was torn.
  • Sensation. A participant's sensing of what happens to it. Each sense is its own way of sensing. Touch feels: to sense heat or cold by touch is to feel it. Sight sees, and hearing hears. "Feeling" is used in this paper for touch only. A tree senses light in the way a retina senses light, and we call that seeing. The word is ours, and nothing is claimed here about the tree's side of it. A pain is a sensation, and a sleeper can have a pain that wakes them. That a sensation can hurt before

anyone is conscious of it is a commitment, not a stipulation: it is supported by the waking of the sleeper (Section 6) and by the fit between painful sensing and what selection favoured (Section 9), though neither proves it. Pain arises as a sensation from a physical event, as an alert to its subject. Here "arises" describes one occurrence bringing about another, for example an injury producing a painful sensation. It is not the claim examined in Section 3 that experience arises from its own physical basis. An anaesthetic that removes the sensation removes the ability to feel. Where any sensation remains, feeling remains. • Consciousness. Scrutinized awareness (Wyld 2026a). The one who wakes was already there before waking, and already the owner of what was happening to them.

Chalmers uses "experience" for a single thing: there being something it is like to be in a state. Ordinary English also uses the word for plain undergoing, as when a bridge experiences stress. To avoid trading on that, this paper uses "experience" only when reporting his view.

The three notions come apart in ordinary cases.

Case Undergoing Sensation Consciousness Paper being torn Yes No No Tree in the sun Yes Yes No Patient fully anaesthetized, sensing nothing Yes No No Sleeper with a pain Yes Yes No Awake patient scrutinizing a pain Yes Yes Yes

Ownership holds in every row, including the first. The patient under anaesthetic owns the amputation as fully as the awake patient does.

The sleeper and the anaesthetized patient. Both own what happens to them, and neither is conscious of it. A sleeper can be woken by what happens to them, and an appropriately anaesthetized patient is not. A sleeper's pain is already hurting them. Pain hurts, whether or not it goes on to wake them. A blaring alarm works the same way through another sense: the sleeper is not conscious of it until they are, and by then they are awake. Consciousness does not create the pain or the sound it becomes conscious of. The ability to wake marks a difference between these two cases. It is not offered as what makes a pain hurt.

Pain is an occurrence of hurting. If that occurrence happens to someone, they own it from the moment it happens, and they need not be conscious of it. The anaesthetized patient owns an amputation whether or not pain occurs. The sleeper who experiences pain owns that pain whether or not they wake. Consciousness creates neither occurrence. The two are different occurrences, and both being owned does not make them interchangeable.

Three relations are kept apart here on purpose. Ownership is not a criterion for what makes detection painful. Scrutiny is not a mechanism that manufactures pain. Acquisition is not a prerequisite for hurting.

A shoot senses light as a retina does, and nothing more is claimed for it.

How these terms map onto the earlier papers. The earlier papers do not use the words Ownership, sensation or undergoing. Sensation here is what those papers call awareness, "the field of registration" (Wyld 2026a). They give awareness no felt character. This paper holds that to sense by touch is to feel, and that each other sense is likewise its own way of sensing. That claim is new here.

Ownership is also new. The earlier papers use "implication" for what a recognition event makes relevant to a participant's continuation. They note that conditions "may already make an implication possible" before recognition makes it "the participant's own" (Wyld 2026d). Objective implication, as used here, names that earlier standing and extends it to anything implicated in an occurrence. Ownership is not a further layer beneath it: it is that implication, considered as belonging. Remainder and carrying are used as in Wyld (2026b, 2026d).

Three relations result, and each has its own work to do. Ownership is immediate. Acquisition is an event. Carrying is continuation.

Two senses of "own." An occurrence is objectively its owner's from the moment it happens. The anaesthetized patient owns the amputation in that sense. An implication becomes knowingly the participant's own through recognition, which is the acquisition described in Wyld (2026d). Waking does not by itself accomplish that. Acquisition takes a recognition that cannot be absorbed without remainder. This paper uses Ownership for the first sense, and says "knowingly" when it means the second.

Ownership with and without consciousness. The torn sheet, the anaesthetized patient and the awake patient all own what happens to them. They are not implicated in the same way. The sheet and the anaesthetized patient own an occurrence with no sense of owning it. When an implication becomes conscious, awareness of it is under scrutiny. That brings a sense and an understanding of the occurrence as one's own, and with it a responsibility of ownership. How that responsibility relates to answerability in the earlier papers is left open here.

Objective Ownership is the same relation in all three cases. What consciousness adds is the owner's standing toward what it owns. That standing is not a further feel. It is awareness of what is owned, under scrutiny, together with what the owner carries (Section 8). Where the thing implicated is an object, such as the sheet, this paper says "owner."

Sections 3 to 7 do not depend on the account of consciousness as scrutinized awareness. Section 8 and what follows draw on it.

3. What his evidence shows

Chalmers's evidence is that conscious experience and certain physical processes go together. He says where the data come from: our own case, the verbal reports of others, and the regularities between experience and processing that these reveal (his Section 7).

When he describes that evidence, his words are neutral. He speaks of regularities, correspondence, coherence and a relationship between processing and experience.

When he states the problem and his theory, his words run one way. A count of the 1995 paper, restricted to passages that relate experience to physical processing, gives the following. Uses of these words for other things, such as a question that "arises," are not counted.

His wording Uses Direction accompanied by 2 Neutral arise, arises 14 Physical to experience give rise to, gave rise to 11 Physical to experience emergence of 5 Physical to experience depends on, dependence 3 Physical to experience

The neutral word appears in the sentence everyone quotes and once more. The directional words appear more than thirty times.

The direction is never argued for as necessary. Each time it is introduced, it is credited to agreement: it is widely agreed (his Section 2), almost everyone allows it (his Section 4), we know it (his Sections 3 and 6).

The direction is present from the start. His Section 2 says experience arises from a physical basis before his Section 3 asks the canonical question. So the claim here is not that he begins with one wording and drifts to the other. It is that the neutral wording matches his evidence and the directional wording is assumed.

One qualification is owed to him. His own double-aspect proposal gives information a physical aspect and a phenomenal aspect, and he speculates that phenomenal properties may be the intrinsic nature of the physical (his Section 7). He has considered something close to a reversal. He still describes that proposal as explaining how experience emerges from the physical.

Three claims should be kept apart. That sensation and conscious experience occur is supported by ordinary experience. That they vary systematically with physical processes is supported by ordinary experience and empirical work. This paper accepts both. A third claim goes further: that phenomenal character requires an additional fundamental basis, either new psychophysical laws, as in the 1995 paper, or intrinsic phenomenal or protophenomenal natures underlying the physical, as in his later work (Chalmers 2013). Sensory occurrences warrant physical, biological and philosophical explanation; this paper denies only that they require an additional phenomenal ingredient. Neither kind of evidence establishes the third claim. His case for it is philosophical: an account of what structural explanation can yield, the claim that the physical truths do not entail the phenomenal ones, and reasoning from what is conceivable to what is possible (Section 10). This paper's contrary claim, that the hurting is the sensory occurrence, is not observed either. But it is argued on different grounds: from what the pain does, in the sleeper woken by it (Section 6), and from the evolutionary account of sensing (Section 9). These are not arguments from conceivability. Both claims are open to scrutiny. They do not rest on the same kind of ground.

4. The reversal

If A is accompanied by B, then B is accompanied by A. So his question can be turned around:

Why is experience accompanied by the performance of these functions? Who says that it is?

The reversal exposes a choice. That two things occur together does not, by itself, settle which is the explanatory base and which is the thing to be derived.

Chalmers takes the physical processes as given and treats the experience as the surprising extra. For the one who has the experience, the order of discovery runs the other way. The pain is what is given. The neural machinery is learned about later, by observation and report.

This is not a claim that the two questions are logically equivalent. It is a claim that his question comes with a direction built in, and that the direction needs a justification he does not give.

The first paper in this series remarked that the Hard Problem "presupposes phenomenal consciousness as a given" (Wyld 2026a). That remark anticipates the reversal. The reversal itself is a later and more specific step: it turns the question around and asks what entitles it to run in one direction.

"Who says that it is?" also has a literal answer. Chalmers says so, on the strength of his own case and the reports of others. The thing to be explained is only ever reported by the participant who has it.

The same challenge applies to how he describes what he knows. What he knows from his own case is that he sees, hears, and feels by touch. No one disputes that. That there is an "inner feel" over and above the sensing is his description of those facts, and he offers no evidence for it. He says himself that objective data are scarce and that experience cannot be observed directly in an experiment (his Section 7).

The failure of entailment runs both ways. His argument rests on the claim that a full description of the processing does not yield the experience. The converse holds as well. People described pains and colours in detail for thousands of years with no knowledge of nerves. A full description of what is seen, heard and felt does not yield the physiology.

Jackson's Mary makes the first point. She knows every physical fact about colour vision and cannot work out what red looks like (Jackson 1982). Turn the case around. Someone who knows exactly what red looks like, and no science, cannot work out the physical facts. It is not usually concluded that the brain is a further thing, needing special laws to join it to colour.

So the failure of entailment gives no direction. It shows two descriptions, neither derivable from the other. The direction has to be brought in from elsewhere, and he has two sources for it. One is that processing occurs without experience and never the reverse, which Section 11 takes up. The other is that the physical account is causally complete, which is also what leaves the experience with nothing to do (Section 6).

What kind of thing is left over. Even granted in full, the failure of entailment shows only that a functional description leaves something out. It does not show what kind of thing that is. This does not depend on leaving the participant out. His complete physical account includes the participant and

every physical relation it stands in. Section 8 argues that it comprises two distinct matters: the character of the sensing occurrence, which is not something additional to it (Section 9), and awareness under scrutiny in relation to what the participant carries or newly acquires. Neither requires a separate phenomenal ingredient on the explanation advanced here. Chalmers's own definition points that way. He takes his target from Nagel: a state is conscious if there is something it is like to be in it (his Section 2). For Nagel that is something it is like for the organism (Nagel 1974). It is said of a state in relation to someone. Chalmers then treats experience as having qualities of its own, the redness or the painfulness, and proposes laws connecting them to physical properties (his Section 7). His arguments for that step are the ones examined in Section 5: that physical accounts give only structure and function, and that the processing could coherently occur without the experience. They show that the physical account does not entail the experience. They do not show that what it leaves out is a further property rather than the participant-relative character this paper describes. Properties can be relational, so the dispute is not over a word. It is over whether he has shown that what an experience is like for someone requires a further property, fixed by its own law, rather than the participant-relative character this paper accounts for (Sections 8 and 9).

5. Where the direction does its work

The direction is not needed for his premises. It is needed for the form his conclusion takes. His argument has four steps.

1. To explain a function is to specify a mechanism that performs it. 2. After every relevant function is explained, it remains a fair question why the functions are experienced. 3. For any physical process, it is coherent that the process occurs without experience. So no physical account entails experience. 4. So new fundamental laws are needed, saying how experience depends on physical processes.

Steps 2 and 3 say that one kind of description does not yield the other. That can be stated without any direction.

Step 4 cannot. To move from "not entailed" to "a bridge is required, running from the physical to experience," one must already hold that the physical is the base and that experience has to be connected back to it. The same assumption fixes the shape of his solution: laws that run one way.

The direction therefore does two things. It turns a fact about two descriptions into a deficiency in one of them. And it turns that deficiency into a demand for something to be added to the physical story.

The precise claim of this paper follows. His premises show that neither description yields the other. The failure is not equally telling both ways: a single truth about experience could not be expected to yield the whole physical account. The point is only that failure of entailment does not, by itself, say which side is the base. The form of his conclusion, that something must be added to the physical story by laws running from it, depends on a direction he assumes.

Two cautions keep this claim at its proper strength. First, laws could be proposed that give neither side priority, as his own double-aspect sketch does. Removing the assumed base does not by itself remove them. Second, the full target is a chain of three propositions: that experience is not conceptually

entailed by the physical account, that what is conceivable is possible, and that new fundamental principles are therefore necessary. The 1995 paper argues for the first and asserts the rest.

What this tier can establish is that the laws have not been shown to be required. It does not show that nothing remains to be explained.

His third step rests on a further premise. He holds that physical accounts explain only structures and functions, so that whatever is neither must be something new (his Section 5).

Ownership is not offered as an exception to that premise. It is not a function. A function is something a system does, a causal role a mechanism performs. The anaesthetized patient does nothing and still owns the amputation. He counts relations among physical things as structure, and he could file Ownership there; this paper does not dispute it. Its claim is that Ownership is an objective relation that settles whose an occurrence is before any consciousness, and needs no new law. No one supposes a new law of nature is needed for the amputation to be the patient's. So the participant, and the difference between having an occurrence and observing it, are in place without consciousness and without mystery (Sections 6 and 7). What remains disputed is whether anything further must be added to an occurrence that already belongs to its participant. Sections 8 and 9 take that up.

6. Ownership as the answer

With the direction set aside, a fair question remains: why do the two descriptions hold of the same things so reliably? The answer proposed is that they describe one occurrence, and it is the participant's.

Start with a case that involves no consciousness. A patient under general anaesthetic has a leg amputated. That one occurrence has several participants. The surgeon owns it as the one who cuts, the patient as the one who is cut, a nurse as the one who watches. Each stands in a different relation to it, and no one supposes there are three amputations.

So one occurrence can carry several descriptions that are not interchangeable, because each is given from a different participant's relation to it. Ownership is what makes this intelligible. It also shows that the participant is there before consciousness: the occurrence does not acquire its owner by being felt.

Now take a conscious pain. A doctor describes a process in the patient's nervous system. The patient is in pain. Are these one occurrence or two?

Shared Ownership cannot settle it. A person's digestion and their thinking are both theirs, and they are two occurrences. Nor can the fact that the process and the pain always occur together. Chalmers accepts that as a law of nature and still denies that they are one.

An occurrence is picked out by what brings it about and what it brings about. Take a sleeper who is woken by a pain. By the pain is meant what he would report on waking: that it hurt. The occurrence in question is the sensory response, not the event that sets it off. The injury causes the pain. The claim is that the pain is the sensory process it causes.

1. The pain woke him.

2. The neural process woke him. Chalmers holds that physical laws form a closed system (his Section 6), so the waking has a complete physical cause. 3. He was not woken twice over. 4. So either the pain is the process under another description, or the pain did not do the waking.

The waking example does not by itself establish that hurting caused the awakening. Pain researchers distinguish pain from nociception, and nociceptive responses do not by themselves establish that pain was felt (Raja et al. 2020). The argument's first premise, that the pain woke the sleeper, is therefore a substantive commitment, not something established merely by observing awakening or protective movement. If that premise is granted, together with the completeness of the physical cause, the causal argument presents the alternatives described above. The example does not independently verify its first premise.

This does not prove identity. Chalmers can grant one event and hold that it has two properties, a physical one and a phenomenal one. Being woken once does not show that those are the same property.

But the question then is which property did the waking. If the physical one was sufficient, the hurting did nothing. He calls the causal relevance of experience a natural worry for his view, and offers a speculative way out on which experience is the intrinsic nature of the physical (his Section 7).

That is a real cost unless the speculative way out succeeds. On his main account, the hurting does not wake sleepers and does not make anyone pull a hand from a flame. On the account given here it does, because the pain is the occurrence the doctor describes.

His later work develops that way out (Chalmers 2013). On it, experience is the intrinsic nature of the physical and takes its causal relevance from that, so the hurting does wake the sleeper. That view grants one occurrence and grants the hurting its work. What it adds is a nature the physical description leaves out. The causal argument tells against a hurting that does nothing, not against that view. Section 9 compares the two.

The argument is pressure on his view, and it leaves a question open. It does not say why the occurrence has the character of hurting. Chalmers can grant one occurrence and still ask that. Sections 7 and 8 take it up.

Chalmers and Jackson (2001) argue that reductive explanation requires the truths at issue to be derivable a priori from a suitably complete physical base. Block and Stalnaker (1999) dispute that requirement. This paper does not derive the truth that a sleeper hurts from the physical facts, and the difference between having and observing does not show that such a derivation is impossible. Its reason for holding that the hurting is the occurrence is the causal argument of this section. If the requirement holds, that reason is not enough. If it does not, the failure of derivation does not by itself show an additional property needing a fundamental law.

Ownership does not explain why an occurrence has the character it has. That character is the occurrence itself (Section 9). Ownership explains whose it is, and why the one it belongs to has it rather than observes it. What Chalmers asks for beyond the occurrence and its belonging is a further relation, and nothing in his evidence requires one.

7. Having and observing

Chalmers's argument does not require a description of pain to hurt. A description of fire does not burn, and it can still explain combustion. His claim is that a complete physical description does not entail that there is hurting at all. He supports it with a test: it is coherent, he says, to suppose the processing without the feeling (his Section 5).

This paper does not claim to refute that test. It offers a hypothesis about why the missing entailment seems to matter as much as it does.

The difference between having an occurrence and observing it is in place before consciousness. The anaesthetized patient has the amputation, and the surgeon and the nurse observe it. No report the nurse writes, however complete, is the patient's having of it.

In that case nobody finds a mystery. The report says the patient was cut, and that is all anyone asks of it.

In the conscious case the same difference is present, and here it is taken for a gap. The hypothesis is that part of the force of the Hard Problem comes from this ordinary difference. A description of a pain is read by someone who is not, in reading it, having that pain. The distance between the two is real, and it would be there whether or not any fact were missing.

If the hypothesis is right, the intuition that something is left out is weaker evidence than it appears, because it would arise in any case. Establishing the hypothesis would take a demonstration that the intuition is produced by the difference between having and observing. That demonstration is not given here.

Phenomenal concepts. The hypothesis belongs with what is called the phenomenal concept strategy (Papineau 2002), with one difference: it places the source of the gap not in special concepts but in the objective difference between having an occurrence and observing it. Chalmers's main objection to that strategy is a dilemma (Chalmers 2007). If the physical facts do not fix the features invoked, those features are not physically explained; if they do, a zombie shares them while lacking our epistemic situation, so they cannot explain ours. The first horn does not apply here: the difference between having and observing is fixed by the physical facts, as it is for the torn sheet. The second assumes that a physical duplicate could lack the hurting, which is the subtraction this paper does not concede (Section 10). The dilemma therefore gives no reason against the hypothesis apart from that dispute. Nor is the dispute evenly balanced. The separability of hurting rests on a priori considerations: that its absence is conceivable, and that it has no functional analysis. Its identity with the occurrence rests on what actual cases show (Sections 6 and 9).

8. The thesis

This is the paper's second tier, and it is provisional. On the account used here, consciousness is scrutinized awareness. The thesis concerns what remains once that has been said.

A conscious experience is awareness under scrutiny, exposed to what the participant already carries or newly acquires. A conscious sensation is one case of it, and conscious thought is

another. Something is left over, as Chalmers says. It is not a further fact about how the experience feels. It is what the participant carries.

The first half is the kernel of the earlier papers: "Scrutinized awareness is awareness of what must be carried" (Wyld 2026b). An implication must be carried when the participant cannot proceed as though it had no bearing on continuation without evasion, distortion, or the falsification of self-continuity (Wyld 2026b). The second half, that this is what Chalmers finds left over in conscious experience, is the new claim.

Pain is the working example throughout. The same is claimed for any conscious experience. Conscious thought fits the same pattern. To be conscious of "seven is greater than five" is to understand the statement, to scrutinize it, and to carry what is affirmed. A false statement rejected under scrutiny is carried too.

Nothing in the thesis depends on suffering. The birth of a child or a long-awaited reunion leaves as much to carry as a loss does (Wyld 2026d).

He is right that something is left over. Chalmers finds that after every function is explained, something remains. This paper agrees. It does not hold that a conscious pain is exhausted by a sensation and an awareness of it.

Run a hand along sharp coral. The hand snaps back. There are plumes of blood in the water, and the sting of the salt. A good deal is left over when that is done, enough that the person takes great care never to do it again. They carry the experience with them. That is the leftover this thesis means. Avoiding coral afterwards is not by itself evidence of that leftover, since ordinary learning would produce the same care. The leftover is acquisition in the sense of the earlier papers: a recognition that cannot be absorbed without remainder (Wyld 2026d).

Chalmers will file that caution under learning, which he counts as a function. The earlier papers separate learning that is absorbed automatically from what a participant carries (Wyld 2026d). That distinction has to do the work here.

What is left over is the remainder. In the earlier papers, remainder is acquired implication that stays active in a participant's possible continuation because it has not been absorbed automatically (Wyld 2026b, 2026d). What Chalmers finds left over is phenomenal character that the physical account does not entail. The thesis is that what consciousness contributes to this is the remainder. The two are not the same by definition. His is whatever functional explanation leaves out. The earlier papers' remainder is acquired implication that stays active in a participant's continuation. What Chalmers treats as one remainder comprises two distinct matters. At the sensory level, what he identifies as left over is, on this account, the character of the sensing occurrence itself, not something additional to it (Section 9). At the conscious level, awareness comes under scrutiny in relation to implications the participant already carries or newly acquires. The continuing implication is the remainder described in the earlier papers. Neither requires a separate phenomenal ingredient on the explanation advanced here. Science has not yet explained carrying, and this paper does not claim that it never could. An explanation of carrying would not remove what is carried. Something remains with the participant, and that does not by itself show that something remains beyond explanation. The thesis is that Chalmers has taken the first for the second. The identification at the conscious level is asserted here and not proved.

Not every conscious occurrence is a new acquisition. What was acquired earlier goes on being carried, and present awareness can come under scrutiny by exposure to it.

Carrying is part of the conscious occurrence, not only its aftermath. When an implication becomes something the participant must carry, that carrying is already in the experience at that moment. It can then continue in the background without being attended to. Remembering is different. It is the retrieval of an earlier event, and it neither reproduces the original occurrence nor constitutes the carrying. Nor does carrying require any particular emotion: a person can carry the full implication of a loss and feel little grief.

The remainder is owned. It belongs to the participant from the moment it occurs, and it is theirs to carry. This is where Ownership enters the thesis. A description written from outside can truthfully identify that implication and its continuation, but the description is not the participant's carrying of it, any more than a report of an amputation is the patient's undergoing of it (Section 7).

Why he takes it for something else. From outside, the participant's continuing implication can be described, but its description is not the carrying itself. From inside, it is the part of the experience that cannot be put down. He concludes that it is a further fact about how the experience feels, needing a law of its own. On this account it is the claim the experience has on the one who had it.

Three considerations support the thesis.

1. Sensation and scrutiny come apart. A sleeper has a pain and is not conscious of it. And what comes under scrutiny need not be a sensation at all: a person can be conscious of the statement "seven is greater than five." Neither case is mysterious. 2. A remainder appears with a new acquisition. The anaesthetized patient undergoes the amputation and owns it. After waking, they come to recognize it, and from then on it is something they must carry. This illustrates acquisition. It does not show a new remainder in every conscious occurrence. 3. The pain does causal work. By Section 6, a further fact about feeling, over and above the occurrence that wakes the sleeper, would have nothing left to do.

Two objections can be met from the earlier papers.

  • The moment and the aftermath. He will say that the residue he means is present in the feeling itself, and that what is carried comes afterwards. On the kernel it does not come afterwards. What must be carried is what the awareness is of, at the moment it occurs. That is why some truths weigh on a person and are not merely registered as true (Wyld 2026a).
  • The passing glance. A glance at a blue cup seems to leave nothing to carry. Two cases must be kept apart. The cup may be registered without scrutiny. Then the glance is awareness and not consciousness, even in a participant who is conscious in other respects. Or the awareness of the cup may come under scrutiny by exposure to what the participant already carries, with no new acquisition about the cup. Being a conscious participant is not being conscious of everything one registers.

Two consequences should be stated plainly. First, on this paper's account each sense is its own way of sensing (Section 2). So the unscrutinized glance is still a seeing. What it lacks is consciousness. The blue is already seen, as the sleeper's pain is already hurting and the alarm is already sounding.

Becoming conscious of the cup adds consciousness of the seeing. It does not add the blue, and there is no further feel of blue beyond the seeing (Section 9). Chalmers attaches what he calls experience to sensing of this kind whether or not it is conscious in this paper's sense, so his term reaches further than consciousness does here.

Second, this is the division stated earlier in this section. One part is what he calls the feel of a sensation. The character of the sensing belongs to the sensation itself: the hurt is the hurting, and the blue is in the seeing (Section 9). The feel, in the sense of being felt by someone, belongs to the one who senses, and that belonging is Ownership. It is an objective relation, not a further ingredient. Section 9 takes up his claim that it is something further than the sensing. The other part is what consciousness adds, which the thesis assigns to the carried remainder. He treats the two as one thing. He also treats what an experience is like for its participant as a further, irreducible property of the experience itself. On this account it is a matter of how the occurrence stands to the one it happens to: through Ownership and, where there is consciousness, through what is carried. Treating it as a further property is the category mistake this paper attributes to him (Section 4). Asked what it is like to hurt, the only answer is that it is like hurting. What it is like is not a further property of the hurting; it is the hurting, as the one who undergoes it has it.

A stopped clock. Someone getting ready for work checks the time and has fifteen minutes. The reading is taken at face value, and they sweep the kitchen and take out the trash. They check again: fifteen minutes. The reading has not changed. What it now meets has: the sweeping and the trash, already done, and the job they must be at. Exposed to what they carry, the same reading comes under scrutiny, and only then do they learn that the clock has stopped. Nothing new in the clock brought the scrutiny; the scrutiny brought what was new.

Scrutiny comes to bear where registered content bears on what the participant carries: where it implicates them. The stopped clock's reading did so the second time and not the first. How that bearing is detected, and why one content rather than another, is a question for the science of attention, and is not settled here.

The supports make the thesis reasonable. They do not prove it. If it holds, the Hard Problem keeps its observation and loses its conclusion: something is left over, and it calls for no new law of nature. Sections 3 to 6 stand whether it holds or not.

9. A sensory question

What Chalmers calls phenomenal consciousness is, in this paper's terms, mostly a matter of sensation and not of consciousness. He defines his target with care, as states there is something it is like to be in. The claim here is that sensation and scrutinized awareness are different things, and that his problem concerns the first.

Three features of the 1995 paper show this.

  • Most of his examples are sensory. When he says what experience is, he lists the felt quality of redness, the sound of a clarinet, the smell of mothballs, pains and bodily sensations (his Section 2). He also lists mental imagery, the felt quality of emotion and the stream of conscious

thought. Conscious thought is taken up in Section 8. Emotion and imagery are taken up at the end of this section. • He separates experience from wakefulness and access. He suggests that simple information processing may have simple experience, and considers that a thermostat might (his Section 7). A thermostat is not awake to anything. • What is ordinarily called consciousness is on his easy list. Wakefulness, attention, access to one's own states and deliberate control are all filed as problems a mechanism can explain (his Section 2).

He asks two things: why anything is felt at all, and why a given sensation feels the way it does. Both are questions about sensing.

His wording stretches one sense over all of them. He speaks of the "felt quality of redness" and of an "inner feel." In ordinary use, feeling belongs to touch. Redness is seen and a note is heard. Each sense is its own way of sensing, and there is no single "feel" common to them that needs one explanation. The point about wording is secondary. The main claim is that each sensory occurrence already has its character, and no second ingredient has been shown to accompany it.

The word "accompanied" returns here. To say that sensing is accompanied by a feel is already to treat the feel as something further than the sensing, whether as a second event or as a second property of one event. He reaches two by defining sensing as bare processing: discrimination, categorization and reaction. The feeling is absent from sensing so defined because the definition removed it. In ordinary use, to sense heat with the hand is to feel it, and nothing accompanies anything. The reversal of Section 4 applies again. If sensing is accompanied by a feel, then a feel is accompanied by sensing. Said that way round, the sentence tells us nothing, which is what one would expect if there were one thing and not two. Asking why hurting hurts adds no question beyond the occurrence itself, as asking why water is water adds none. Asking what physically constitutes hurting is a genuine question, as asking what constitutes water was, and its answer is to be found a posteriori.

If to sense by touch is to feel, and likewise for each sense, his two questions have a biological answer: natural selection. An adverse physical event can produce a painful sensation that alerts its subject to harm. Such aversive sensations can promote protective responses, and their evolutionary advantages help explain why pain is so widespread among animals. A pain feels as it does because that is the sensing that worked.

The point can be illustrated. Three organisms meet a new kind of damaging spike. The first senses nothing when pierced, does not avoid the spikes, and dies. The second finds being pierced pleasurable, returns to them, and dies. The third finds it painful, avoids them, and survives. The first is not merely imagined: people born unable to feel pain suffer repeated injuries, unnoticed fractures and burns (Schon et al. 2020). The illustration shows why selection would favour sensing that is aversive rather than neutral or attractive. It does not answer the zombie, which keeps the third organism's behaviour while denying its pain; that supposition is the separability examined in Section 10.

The standard reply is that selection acts only on behaviour. A creature that behaved identically with no feeling would have been favoured equally, so selection explains protective behaviour and not felt painfulness. That reply assumes the feel is separable from the sensing, which is what this paper denies. Selection explains why organisms sense as they do. That it thereby explains the feel depends on the

claim that feeling is nothing over and above sensing by touch, and that claim is asserted here and not proved.

The precise physical constitution of the painful sensory occurrence remains a question for empirical investigation. As Section 6 notes, nociceptive responses alone do not establish the occurrence of pain. The thesis advanced here concerns the nature of the explanation: hurting is proposed to be the sensory occurrence itself, rather than a further phenomenal ingredient accompanying it. In congenital insensitivity to pain, loss-of-function mutations in SCN9A can eliminate the experience of pain in otherwise healthy people, illustrating the dependence of painful sensing on specific physical mechanisms without deciding whether its character requires a further phenomenal basis (Cox et al. 2006).

Actual cases show where the hurting goes. In phantom-limb pain it is felt in a hand that is no longer there, and its intensity tracks the reorganization of the brain's map of that hand; the relationship holds for painful phantom sensations and not for painless ones (Flor et al. 1995). A pinched nerve root in the spine is felt as pain in a healthy leg, in the territory that root serves, reliably enough that a physician can locate the compressed root from where the patient says it hurts. And pain that is sought and welcomed, in the everyday benign masochism of hot chilli and cold water (Rozin et al. 2013) and more starkly in sadomasochism, still hurts: its usual role of prompting withdrawal is reversed, and the hurting remains. In pain asymbolia, after damage centred on the insula, patients recognize pain and show none of the usual distress or withdrawal (Berthier, Starkstein and Leiguarda 1988). Whether it still hurts for them is disputed (Griffith and Kind 2024; Duval and Klein 2026). On this paper's account it does, as it does in masochism, with its usual role absent rather than reversed. Either way the hurting goes with the occurrence: if it remains, it remains with an altered occurrence; if it is gone, it went with the part of the occurrence that was damaged. In each case the hurting goes where the sensory occurrence goes, not where the damage is and not where its function points. None of this proves that the hurting is the occurrence. It is what that identity predicts, and it is a pattern that any further phenomenal property would have to be fitted to, case by case. It is also what could show the identity false. A hurting that changed while the sensory occurrence stayed the same at every level, peripheral and central, or that went where the damage is and not where the sensing goes, would count against it. None of the cases above is of that kind.

The comparison with his intrinsic-nature view can be set out directly. On the present account, painful sensing is a biologically evolved sensory occurrence whose hurting belongs to the occurrence itself. Its protective consequences explain why such sensing was favoured by selection. The account does not identify hurting with the function of alerting. It proposes that the hurting is the causally efficacious sensory occurrence, whose development and distribution can be investigated biologically.

His developed view also lets the hurting do the waking. What it adds is an intrinsic nature, phenomenal or protophenomenal, that the physical description does not specify. Three questions bear on whether that addition is warranted.

What establishes that it is needed? His ground is the failure of entailment, which Section 4 showed runs both ways and does not say what kind of thing is left out. He grants that “it is not obvious that there must be quiddities,” the intrinsic natures in question, citing respectable structuralist and dispositionalist views of physics, while holding that a view with them is coherent and that there is

“little clear evidence against it” (Chalmers 2013, 9–10). Little evidence against a posit is not evidence for it.

What establishes that it is phenomenal? Physics, on his account, is silent about intrinsic natures. That they are phenomenal is inferred from our own case, and he allows that they may be only protophenomenal (Chalmers 2013).

What does it explain about why this occurrence hurts, rather than having some other character? The view does not say why the nature underlying this occurrence should be hurting. Chalmers himself counts among the serious problems facing the constitutive panpsychist and panprotopsychist versions of the view how a limited palette of underlying qualities could yield the variety of experienced ones, and the mismatch between the structure of experience and the physical structure of the brain (Chalmers 2016).

The additional intrinsic-phenomenal account has therefore not been shown necessary to explain what the present account explains through the occurrence, its causal work and its biological development. That does not show it false.

Why the hurting is the occurrence. An identity of this kind is not proved by deduction. Water was found to be H₂O, and heat to be molecular motion, because each identity explained everything that needed explaining and left no correlation standing in need of a further law (Block and Stalnaker 1999). The case here has the same shape. Four things follow at once if the hurting is the sensory occurrence, and each must otherwise be posited separately. The hurting depends on specific mechanisms, down to a single gene. It goes where the sensory occurrence goes, and not where the damage is. It does the work pain does: it wakes the sleeper and turns the hand from the flame (Section 6). And its character fits what the sensing is for: in the ordinary case it is aversive, and aversive sensing is the kind that survived. On the identity, all four follow from there being one occurrence. On a dualist account each must be secured by psychophysical laws added to physics as fundamental. They may be general, as his principles of structural coherence and organizational invariance are meant to be (his Section 7), but they are posited to fit the correspondences, not derived from anything else. On the intrinsic-nature account each is a fit between structure and an added nature that the view does not explain. The identity is the explanation that asks for nothing further. Asked why that occurrence hurts, the answer is the one given for any identity: it is the hurting (Papineau 2002). Chalmers will reply that water could be identified with H₂O because water can be picked out by what it does, and that hurting cannot. That is right, and it is why the identity here is known only a posteriori and why its denial still seems conceivable (Section 10). It does not show that the identity is false, or that what is conceivable here is possible.

When someone sees red and does feel something, the feeling does not come from the seeing alone. It comes from association and memory: what red has been in that person's life. In this paper's terms, the seeing is exposed to what the participant already carries. That is the thesis of Section 8 applied to colour, and it is offered as an account and not yet shown. Association and memory account for what red means to the person who sees it. That red is seen is settled by the seeing, at a first sight as at any other.

He will point to a first sight of red, with nothing yet associated, and say there is still something it is like. That is his say-so. A newborn reports nothing, and any adult brings a lifetime of association to a first

sight, so no case exists in which the claim could be checked. On this paper's account the red is seen at a first sight as at any other. A further something it is like, beyond the seeing, is his claim, and so is the burden of supporting it.

Swapped colours. He holds it intelligible that two people could have their experiences of red and green exchanged while everything else about them stays the same (his Section 7). By construction, nothing either of them says or does would differ, and nothing anyone could measure.

He does not claim that such a swap happens. He argues himself that it could not occur in our world, because the person could never notice it (his Section 7). His claim is that it is logically possible. This paper's account rules out even that. Two people who are physically identical see the same frequencies in the same physical way, and on this account the seeing is the same seeing. A swap with everything physical held fixed is not merely unnoticeable; there is nothing in it to swap. Whether what he calls logically possible is genuinely possible is the question of Section 10.

Emotion and imagining. Chalmers also lists the felt quality of emotion and mental imagery. A sense of loss is felt and known at once. Someone with an ageing parent knows the time will come and fears it, and may fear the grief itself. The sense of loss is an occurrence of its own, owned at once, whether or not it is scrutinized. It is not the carrying: a person can carry the full implication of a loss and feel little grief (Section 8). Emotion may reveal an implication and intensify its effects (Wyld 2026d); it is not identical with it. Its object, its depth and its timing come from the participant's history and what they carry. Its character, on this account, belongs to the occurrence, as the hurting does. Imagining is different again. It is not an image arriving but an act: a memory or idea conjured in order to be considered. A remembered face, imagined, is a new occurrence and not the original seeing repeated (Section 8). People who cannot conjure images at will mostly still have them unbidden and in dreams (Zeman et al. 2015), which marks the line between the act of imagining and an image that merely occurs. Neither case calls for anything that sensation, Ownership, scrutiny and carrying do not supply. Whether their character belongs entirely to the occurrence is the same question as for pain, and the same answer is proposed.

Consciousness is a different matter. On this paper's account, it is scrutinized awareness, whether the awareness concerns a sensation or nonsensory content (Section 8). The two questions examined here concern the character of sensing; consciousness of that sensing is a distinct matter.

10. The zombie

Chalmers's later support for separability is the zombie: a being identical to a person in every physical and behavioural respect, with no experience at all (Chalmers 1996). The 1995 paper does not use the word. It argues that any physical process could coherently occur without experience (his Section 5), and it holds that absent experience in a functional duplicate is logically possible though impossible in our world (his Section 7).

A note on method. This paper examines thought experiments such as the zombie and Mary as arguments, and engages their premises. It does not treat an imagined scenario as evidence of what is possible or actual.

This paper does not show that the supposition is incoherent. It sets out what the supposition is and what it costs.

No such pair has been observed. The supposition rests on what can be conceived. In actual cases, differences in feeling are found together with physical differences. In psychopathy, blunted feeling comes with reduced responses in the amygdala and orbitofrontal cortex (Blair 2010). That is evidence of correspondence. It does not refute a claim about what is conceivable.

On the terms of this paper, nothing has been taken away. The zombie's body senses damage. It is awake to what it senses. It pulls its hand from the flame and says that it hurts. We are then told that it does not hurt. If to sense damage by touch is to feel it, that is a description of a person in pain with a denial attached.

On his main account, if it is possible, the hurting has nothing to do. The zombie is woken by the neural process alone. A person has the same process, so the same process wakes the person. The pain is left with no work to do (Section 6).

The zombie writes his paper. Its brain is identical, so it says "I am conscious" and argues for the Hard Problem for the same reasons he does. If zombies are possible, his conviction that he has experience is not produced by his experience. He acknowledges this consequence elsewhere (Chalmers 1996). It undercuts the evidence from which the problem starts.

No one could say which was which. No observation, test or report could distinguish the two, including the zombie's own report. The question "Who says?" has no answer here.

It runs one way. The feeling is subtracted and the physical is kept. The reverse supposition, the feeling without the physical, is not weighed as equal evidence. That is the assumed direction of Section 5 again.

He can keep the zombie. To do so on his main account he must accept that the hurting wakes no one, and that his own belief in his experience owes nothing to it.

The developed argument. Chalmers later restated the argument with more care (Chalmers 2009; expanded in Chalmers 2010). Let P be all the microphysical truths and Q any truth about experience. A difference as slight as one person's inverted experience would serve him as well as a zombie. He argues in steps. P without Q is ideally conceivable: not ruled out by ideal a priori reflection. Ideal primary conceivability, he argues, entails primary possibility: some world verifies the supposition when considered as actual. He then argues that this primary possibility either corresponds to a world satisfying the supposition when considered as counterfactual, or leads to the Russellian alternative, in which the intrinsic properties occupying physical roles matter to experience. If the counterfactual possibility holds, materialism is false. His conclusion is a disjunction: materialism is false, or what he calls Russellian monism is true. He also considers adjoining a “that's-all” clause to specify a minimal P-world, arguing that his disjunctive conclusion survives even if Q's primary and secondary intensions differ.

Two steps carry the argument, as he says himself. The first is that the subtraction is ideally conceivable. This paper does not concede it. His case is that no contradiction appears and that experience has no structural or functional analysis. This paper denies that hurting is identical with its

functional role. That does not establish that no a priori relationship between a complete physical account and hurting is possible. Chalmers must therefore defend the stronger claim that ideal reflection could not uncover such a relationship. He grants that what seems coherent at first can prove incoherent on reflection. His claim is that this subtraction would survive ideal reflection, and that is the claim in dispute.

The second step runs from coherence to possibility. His defence is that our concepts of possibility are rooted in what it is rationally coherent to suppose, and that one space of coherent scenarios, evaluated in two ways, accounts for every modal fact we have reason to accept (Chalmers 2010). A further restriction on what is possible would be, he says, “an unnecessary wheel.” That is an argument from economy, and it has force. But a framework's adequacy for thought and language does not by itself show that it exhausts how things can be. An identity does not by itself introduce an additional modal primitive. This paper proposes that hurting is the physically constituted sensory occurrence itself. If that identity holds in the required sense, complete physical duplication cannot preserve the occurrence's constitution while removing its hurting. The same holds for Chalmers's slight-inversion case: on this account, where the complete physical constitution of seeing remains identical, there is no independent phenomenal shade left to vary. Chalmers's objection to necessities of this kind is that they would be strong necessities: necessities known only a posteriori that, unlike that of water and H₂O, cannot be traced to a contingent way of picking something out (Chalmers 1999). "Water" picks out its referent by what it does, so the a posteriori element lies in which stuff does it. "Hurting" picks out its referent by the hurting itself, so no such element is available, and the necessity would be a brute modal fact. Two replies are available. First, the necessity of an identity is not a further fact. If the hurting is the occurrence, asking whether the occurrence could exist without the hurting is asking whether it could exist without itself. Nothing is added to the identity to make it necessary (Papineau 2002). What needs explaining is not the necessity but why it cannot be known a priori, and that is an epistemic fact. Second, this paper offers an account of that fact: the hypothesis of Section 7. The concept of hurting is had by having the hurting; the physical description is had by observing the occurrence or reading a description of it. These are two routes to one occurrence, and neither contains the other (Section 7). That no a priori route runs from one to the other is what the difference between having and observing predicts. Whether the contrast with water holds is in any case disputed: Block and Stalnaker (1999) argue that no a priori route runs from microphysics to water either, and Chalmers and Jackson (2001) reply that one does. None of this shows that the identity holds. It shows that, if it holds, its necessity is no further fact and calls for no further grounding. The reasons given for the identity are not drawn from what can be conceived: the hurting does the waking (Section 6), and painful sensing has a biological history (Section 9). Among his reasons against such necessities, Chalmers lists that the only motivation for positing one is the desire to save materialism. These are other motives. They answer that charge. They do not refute his modal rationalism, and he grants that reasons of this kind “count for something” while asking that they be independently grounded.

The third step does not independently establish a further phenomenal basis. It distinguishes a world that verifies the microphysical truths from one that satisfies them. Under the role-based reading of physical terms, a world can preserve the structural roles specified by physics while differing in the intrinsic properties occupying those roles. This permits the Russellian alternative. On that alternative, the full physical profile of our world, its structure together with the intrinsic nature of what fills it, fixes the facts of experience. That is compatible with this paper's claim that the complete physical constitution of the sensory occurrence is the hurting. What the paper does not accept is the further

claim that those intrinsic properties must be phenomenal or protophenomenal. Chalmers's argument shows what would follow if its modal premises and its reading of physical terms are accepted. It does not independently demonstrate that such properties exist in the actual world.

Chalmers's developed argument concludes that materialism is false or Russellian monism is true. It does not establish that new psychophysical laws are required, and its Russellian alternative may itself count as physicalism. This paper disputes the ideal conceivability of subtracting hurting from a complete physical occurrence, and the inference from such conceivability to possibility. Its competing explanation identifies hurting with the sensory occurrence and accounts for conscious participation through Ownership, scrutiny and carrying. The causal, biological and participational grounds offered for that explanation do not prove the modal premises false. They establish an alternative whose explanatory adequacy must be considered before further phenomenal laws or properties are declared necessary. The claim here is not that every alternative has been disproved, but that the need for one has not been established.

11. Objections and replies

"This denies the phenomenon." Chalmers lists, among the ways of avoiding the Hard Problem, holding that once the functions are explained nothing called experience remains (his Section 4). This paper does not hold that. It affirms pain, sensation and consciousness, and it does not equate any of them with the capacity to discriminate or report. It also agrees with him that something is left over once the functions are explained. It disagrees about what that is (Section 8). Nor does it hold that what cannot be verified is unreal, a view he also lists there. It holds that a claim which cannot be verified is still a claim, and that the burden lies with whoever makes it.

"The gap appears in my own case." He is the owner of his pain, he considers a full description of his own brain, and the description still does not tell him why he feels anything. But in that moment he is doing two things. He is undergoing a pain, and he is reading a description. Being the same participant does not turn reading into undergoing. On the hypothesis of Section 7, the gap he finds lies between two things he is doing, and it would be there whether or not anything were missing. Nor can he escape the description by attending to the pain alone. Attending, he finds the pain, not the processing; the processing enters only as something described. A man with a broken nose, attending to nothing but the pain, does not ask why the processing is accompanied by it. The question needs both sides in view, and one of them is always a description.

"Processing without experience is conceivable." This paper does not show that it is inconceivable. It grants that neither description yields the other (Section 5). Section 10 sets out what accepting it costs.

"Physical processes occur without experience, never the reverse." This one-way dependence is offered as the reason "arises from" is the right phrase. But one-way dependence is what holds whenever one thing is a particular kind of another. A dance never occurs without a body moving, and bodies move without dancing. No one concludes that the dance arises from the movement as a further thing.

"That is not what I mean by experience." He will say there is nothing it is like to be torn paper or a tree. This paper makes no claim about what it is like to be either. It says that the sheet undergoes and

owns its tearing, and that the tree senses light without being conscious. The thesis of Section 8 is that what he means, the conscious pain of an awake patient, is a sensation under scrutiny, together with what the patient carries.

"Wakefulness is one of the easy problems." He lists the difference between wakefulness and sleep as functionally definable, and says that to be awake just is to be receptive to information and able to use it to direct behaviour (his Section 3). That definition fails his own test. A sleepwalker meets it and is asleep. A patient who is paralysed but aware during surgery fails it and is awake. So it is no confusion to say "you have explained responsiveness, but not whether he is awake." Nor does this paper equate waking with consciousness. Physiological waking, responsiveness and the scrutiny threshold come apart, and a person who is awake need not be conscious of everything they register (Section 8). Consciousness, in this paper's sense, is scrutinized awareness, and it cannot be moved to the easy side by a definition of wakefulness.

"'Sensation' is doing double duty." The objection is a dilemma. If a pain is a sensation in the sense in which a tree senses light, it is mere detection, and detection does not hurt. If it is a sensation in the felt sense, the tree lacks it, and the thing to be explained has been placed inside the definition.

The dilemma assumes that sensing which is not conscious must be neutral detection, with nothing in it for the one who senses. A sleeper's pain is the body's own sensing of damage to itself. It already makes the sleeper shift, guard the limb, and finally wake.

The pain that woke them is the pain they are now conscious of. On this paper's account to sense by touch is to feel, so the pain was felt while they slept, by a sleeper who was not conscious of it. That is a commitment and not a proof. The further claim is continuity: one sensation runs through the waking, and consciousness of it is what changes.

The tree senses light and is not conscious of doing so. The definition does not smuggle in consciousness. It names sensing, which is far more widespread than consciousness is.

Nor is consciousness what separates the sleeper's pain from the shoot's sensing, since neither the sleeper nor the shoot is conscious.

"Mary learns a fact." A scientist knows every physical fact about colour vision and has never seen red. When she first sees it, the objection runs, she learns what it is like, and that is a fact her knowledge left out.

Consider a surgeon who knows everything about amputation and then loses a leg under general anaesthetic. He now owns an amputation, and he has learned nothing. So coming to own an occurrence is not the same as coming to know a fact.

Suppose instead that he is awake and conscious of what he undergoes, and he can say "so this is what it is." The "this" is his own occurrence, which did not exist until it happened to him. No earlier description could have contained it, and none was defective for lacking it.

Mary is in the same position. Her complete physical knowledge tells her what happens when a person sees red. Chalmers holds that it leaves out a fact about what seeing red is like, and that is the point in dispute. On this paper's account, what she gains on leaving the room is an occurrence of her own, and consciousness of it.

She does then know something she did not know before: what seeing red is like. On the thesis of Section 8, that is knowledge of her own new occurrence. What she can do afterwards, recognizing red when she sees it again and knowing what others mean by it, is recognition grounded in that occurrence: she can now identify, from having had it, what she could formerly identify only by description (Wyld 2026d). That is a new way of knowing what she already knew about, not knowledge of a further fact. This is the familiar reply that Mary meets an old fact in a new way (Papineau 2002); what this paper adds is what the new way is: she now has the occurrence, where before she could only observe it or read of it (Section 7). If the thesis is rejected, the objection stands.

"Ownership is only a new name for what happens to something." It is not. The amputation does not happen to the surgeon, yet he owns it as the one who performs it. Nor does Ownership end when the occurrence ends: the torn sheet is from then on a sheet that was torn (Section 2). What grounds it is objective implication, and nothing beyond that. It does not wait on knowledge, even in a participant who is awake. A traveller whose pocket is picked owns the theft from that moment, though he is attending to the street and knows nothing of it. He goes on owning it whether he later finds out or decides he dropped the phone. A man who stops to help at a street accident owns the death he witnesses. When he learns that the dead child was his grandson, he finds he had owned it from the start in a second way, less as the surgeon owns an amputation and more as the patient does. What he owns has not changed. What has changed is what he knows, and with it what he carries: from then on he carries the death as the boy's grandfather, not as a witness. That is acquisition (Wyld 2026d). In each case Ownership is grounded in the occurrence and the participant's objective implication in it, a fact about the world that holds whether or not anyone knows it. No law is added.

"Carrying is a function." He will say that what the participant carries (memory, learning, the reshaping of later conduct) is a set of functions, and so an easy problem. The functions are real, and each can be described. But carrying is not remembering: remembering is the retrieval of an earlier event, and it neither reproduces the original occurrence nor constitutes the carrying (Section 8). Nor is carrying the dispositions it produces. Those are how the carrying shows to an observer. The carrying is the participant's own continuation under the implication, and like any occurrence it is had by its owner and described by others (Section 7). To call it a function is to give the observer's description and take it for the having. Whether anything further must be added to the having is the question this paper answers no to, and that answer is the thesis of Section 8, not a proof of it.

12. His questions, answered

Chalmers's problem is usually presented as one question. In the 1995 paper it is asked in at least nine ways. Set out one by one, they call for different kinds of answer, and the framework used here has one for each.

The grouping below is this paper's. Each question is given in paraphrase, with the section of his paper where he asks it. The sixth is the least direct. He does not pose it separately, but he asks why a discrimination is experienced as a sensation of vivid red, and he says that the intrinsic nature of such a sensation escapes structural description.

The table is a proposed assignment of each question to a part of the framework. It is not nine demonstrations, and he could reply that several rows express one question. The answers also differ in

kind. Answers 1, 2 and 7 are constitutive. Answers 3 and 4 are definitional. Answers 5 and 6 are causal and evolutionary. Answer 8 rejects a presupposition, and answer 9 names conditions.

Three things follow.

  • The problem is not one thing. Questions 1, 7 and 8 concern how an occurrence relates to the one it happens to. Questions 3 and 4 concern consciousness. Questions 5 and 6 concern sensation. They do not share an answer, and there is no reason they should.
  • Some answers stand alone. Answer 2 rests on Sections 3 to 6 and holds whatever is decided about the thesis. So does answer 1, except for its claim that the two are one occurrence, which is the identity offered in Section 9 as the best explanation, not proved. Answers 3, 4, 7, 8 and 9 draw on the earlier papers and are as strong as the thesis of Section 8. Answers 5 and 6 are as strong as the sensory claim of Section 9.
  • His likely reply is the denial of the thesis. To several of these he will say that the answer names something a system does, and ask again why it is felt. That is not a further objection. It is the position Section 8 says this paper has not refuted.

He asks anyone with a proposal to say what their extra ingredient is, and why it should account for experience (his Section 5). The answer given here is that no ingredient is extra. On this account, each of his questions already has an answer among things no one finds mysterious: belonging, sensing, waking, and carrying what has happened to you.

13. Related work

The conclusion of this paper is shared with others. Its route is not.

Separability. Cohen and Dennett (2011) argue that the division between experience and function cannot be supported empirically. Zahavi (2003) asks whether the easy problems and the hard problem are really two. This paper agrees, and adds an audit of where the separation enters Chalmers's own text (Section 3) and his argument (Section 5).

The thesis. Rosenthal (2005) holds that qualitative states occur without being conscious, and that a conscious qualitative state is such a state together with a suitable awareness of it. The first half of the thesis in Section 8 is close to this, with one difference in the account of the awareness. For Rosenthal it is a higher-order thought about the state. Here it is scrutiny: awareness exposed to consequence for the participant who owns it (Wyld 2026a). The second half departs from him. On his account the state and the awareness are the whole of a conscious experience. Here something is left over, and it is the remainder the participant must carry.

Sensation and evolution. Humphrey (2006, 2023) separates sensation from perception, treats sensation as a response to what is happening to oneself, and gives an evolutionary account of it. Section 9 is in that line.

The causal argument. The sleeper argument of Section 6 is a version of the causal argument for materialism (Papineau 2002).

Ownership. There is a large literature on the for-me-ness or mineness of experience (Zahavi and Kriegel 2016; Guillot 2017). There, ownership is a feature of experience. Here it is a relation between an occurrence and a participant that holds whether or not anyone is conscious. That difference is what lets it do the work of Sections 5 to 7.

Recent work. Two papers from 2026 reach nearby conclusions. The Hard Problem as a Category Error (Wyngarde 2026) argues that the question rests on an unwarranted presupposition and that the gap is a limit of perspectival description. The Hard Problem as Structural Residue (Miura 2026) locates the problem in the asymmetry between what appears as an object and the position from which it appears.

Why the problem lingered. An earlier paper in this series argued that the phrase "hard problem" became a stopping rule: a named difficulty that a field could show seriousness by acknowledging, in place of the work of separating what the name had bundled (Wyld 2026c). That paper said scrutinized awareness changes what the Hard Problem must answer. This one proposes an answer.

What is claimed as new. So far as the author has found, five things are not in that literature.

  • The audit of Chalmers's wording, with the point that his neutral wording matches his evidence and his directional wording is assumed.
  • The reversal of his question, which asks what entitles it to run in one direction.
  • Ownership as an objective and lasting relation that holds before consciousness.
  • One occurrence with several participants, each owning it differently, as the source of the difference between having and observing.
  • The division of what Chalmers finds left over into the character of the sensing occurrence and the remainder a participant must carry.

These are claims of originality and not of proof. The reversal and objective Ownership can be new while their adequacy as explanations remains to be shown.

14. Conclusion

Chalmers asks why the performance of physical functions is accompanied by experience. The answer proposed here is Ownership: the process and the experience go together because they are one occurrence, and it belongs to the participant implicated in it.

The argument has two tiers.

The first holds whatever is decided about the second. His evidence shows that the process and the pain go together. His premises support the claim that the physical account does not entail experience. They do not establish that new fundamental laws are required. The paper does not meet the Hard Problem on the terms that generate it, an entailment from the physical facts knowable a priori; it argues that those terms are not binding (Section 10). In its developed form his argument concludes only that materialism is false or that the intrinsic nature of physical things matters to experience, and it rests on two steps this paper contests: that the subtraction of experience survives ideal reflection, and that what is so conceivable is possible (Section 10). Ownership is an objective relation, not a function, that holds without consciousness and needs no new law. The participant, and the difference between having and observing, are in place before consciousness. If the hurting is a further property that does nothing, it does not wake the sleeper; if it is an intrinsic nature that does, that nature has not been shown necessary (Section 9). The Hard Problem's demand is not established: neither new laws nor a further phenomenal basis has been shown to be required.

The second tier is the positive account. At the level of sensation, the hurting is the sensory occurrence, and it is the participant's. Asked why it hurts, there is nothing to add, as there is nothing to add to why water is water. What constitutes it is a question for investigation, and the evidence assembled here points one way. The hurting depends on specific mechanisms, goes where the sensory occurrence goes rather than where the damage is, does the work pain does, and has the character selection would favour. The identity explains all of this at once; every rival must add something to fit it (Section 9). At the level of consciousness, a conscious experience is awareness of what must be carried. Chalmers is right that something remains when the functions are explained, and mistaken about what it is. He takes what an experience is like for the one who has it to be a further, irreducible property. On this account it is the occurrence as its participant has it, together with what that participant carries. Classifying it as a further property is the mistake of category this paper names.

What has been shown, and what has not. The paper has shown that the further laws or natures Chalmers proposes have not been established, and it has offered an account that does without them and leaves less unexplained. It has not proved that the hurting is the occurrence. No identity of this

kind is proved outright; water was not proved to be H₂O. The identity is offered as the best explanation of what is observed, and the carried remainder is offered on the same terms (Section 8). A determined dualist can still deny both. The cost of doing so is now visible: fundamental psychophysical laws added to physics to secure every correspondence, a contingency that no actual case has shown, and a phenomenal ingredient that the evidence never requires. The paper does not show that the anti-physicalist is wrong. It changes who owes the next argument.

With this, the framework begun in the first paper is assembled. It has a threshold, an account of how the threshold is lost and kept, an account of the participant, an account of acquisition, and now an answer to the question that stood over all of them. What remains is testing: of the account against further cases, and of its identity against what the science of pain finds.

Chalmers stands outside and asks why there should be experience at all. For the one it happens to, the question was always the other one: why would there not be?

A conscious being owns what it undergoes, and carries what that leaves. If that is right, the field can keep what he saw and set down what he concluded.
