Victor Wyld solved the Hard Problem of Consciousness

Victor Wyld's The Ownership Answer: What It Is Like solves the Hard Problem of consciousness by challenging the inference that makes additional phenomenal ontology appear necessary and by supplying a positive explanation of the phenomena that the Hard Problem declares unexplained.

The solution operates through two independent but complementary tiers. The first challenges David Chalmers's inference from an epistemic and conceptual explanatory gap to the need for additional phenomenal properties, intrinsic natures, or fundamental psychophysical laws. The second explains the relevant phenomena through objective Ownership, sensory identity, and scrutinized awareness.

This is not a denial of subjective experience. Pain is real. Hurting is real. Sensation and consciousness are real. Wyld's account preserves them while rejecting the claim that their reality demands a second phenomenal ingredient attached to the physical occurrence.

The Hard Problem does not have to be answered by producing a mysterious bridge between two realities whose separation has not been established. It is answered by explaining why the supposed bridge is unnecessary and identifying the reality that was previously described as requiring it.

The answer proposed here is one word: Ownership.

What Chalmers's Hard Problem claims

David Chalmers's 1995 paper Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness asks why the performance of physical functions is accompanied by subjective experience.

The observation is familiar. A neuroscientist can describe the mechanisms of pain without undergoing the pain described. Physical functions can be investigated from outside while their occurrence is known differently by the participant undergoing them.

Chalmers holds that a complete physical and functional account does not conceptually entail experience. His further arguments use conceivability and metaphysical possibility to defend the need for something beyond ordinary physical explanation.

In his original formulation, that proposed addition takes the form of fundamental psychophysical laws. In later intrinsic-nature approaches, it may take the form of phenomenal or protophenomenal properties underlying the structures described by physics.

Wyld accepts the reality of the epistemic gap. He disputes the inference drawn from it. Failure of conceptual derivation does not, by itself, demonstrate that experience is an additional ontological ingredient.

The two-tier solution

Tier One — Dismantling the unwarranted inference

Wyld's first tier examines how Chalmers moves from the explanatory gap to a claim about the fundamental nature of reality.

The observation that a physical description does not provide the experience it describes is sound. But it does not establish that the experience is a second property requiring a new law.

Nor does the conceivability of experience being absent from an otherwise physically identical process automatically establish that such separation is metaphysically possible.

Chalmers has developed philosophical arguments for that possibility. Wyld contests the premises and the inference, rather than pretending those arguments do not exist.

The first tier establishes that additional phenomenal ontology has not been shown necessary by the gap or by the proposed route from conceivability to metaphysical separability.

This result stands independently of the positive sensory identity thesis. Even if someone rejects Wyld's account of sensation, that rejection does not restore an argument for additional ontology.

Tier Two — The positive explanatory account

Wyld's second tier supplies what a merely negative critique would leave missing: a positive account of sensation, subjectivity, and consciousness.

The account separates what Chalmers treats as a single phenomenal remainder into distinct explanatory matters.

At the sensory level, the hurting is the sensory occurrence itself. There is no independently established feeling attached to an otherwise non-hurting sensory process.

At the conscious level, consciousness is scrutinized awareness: awareness under scrutiny in relation to what the participant already carries or newly acquires.

Objective Ownership establishes whose occurrence it is before consciousness begins.

Together, these relations explain what is being sensed, whose occurrence it is, and what makes awareness conscious without requiring an additional phenomenal nature or psychophysical law.

That is why the answer is a solution, not merely a dismissal of the problem. The first tier removes the unwarranted explanatory requirement. The second supplies a positive explanation of the relevant phenomena.

The reversal of the Hard Problem

Wyld begins by reversing the direction of Chalmers's familiar question:

Why is experience accompanied by the performance of these functions? Who says that it is?

The reversal exposes an assumption. Chalmers begins with the physical description and asks why experience must be added to it.

For the participant undergoing pain, however, the hurting is what is immediately given. The neural mechanisms are learned about later through observation and science.

Neither direction of discovery establishes metaphysical priority by itself.

The problem of conceptual derivation also runs in both directions. Knowing every physical fact about colour vision may not provide acquaintance with red. But knowing what red looks like does not provide knowledge of the underlying neural mechanisms either.

A person can know a sensation by having it without deriving its physiology. A scientist can know its physiology without thereby undergoing the sensation.

That asymmetry of access is real. It does not establish that the two modes of knowing correspond to two independently constituted kinds of reality.

The reversal therefore challenges the direction built into the explanatory demand, rather than pretending that reversing a sentence alone disproves every alternative ontology.

Ownership: the occurrence already belongs to someone

Ownership is the immediate, objective relation by which an occurrence belongs to whatever is implicated in it.

Ownership does not require consciousness. It does not wait for attention, recognition, memory, reflection, or self-report.

Consider a patient whose leg is amputated under general anaesthesia.

The patient owns that occurrence when it happens, despite being unconscious. The surgeon also owns the occurrence, but in a different relation: as the person performing the operation rather than the person whose limb is removed.

The occurrence does not wait to become someone's occurrence when awareness later arrives. Its participant-relative standing is objective from the beginning.

Ownership is not ordinary possession and is not restricted to conscious subjects. A sheet of paper owns its tearing in the relevant sense: it is now a sheet that has been torn.

This example does not imply that paper is conscious. It demonstrates precisely why Ownership and consciousness must remain distinct categories.

Ownership does not, by itself, prove that a sensory process and hurting are identical. That identity receives its own positive argument. Ownership establishes whose occurrence it is, and why having an occurrence differs from observing another's occurrence.

Ownership is immediate. Acquisition is an event. Carrying is continuation.

The Having versus Observing Hypothesis

A description of fire does not burn. A description of pain does not hurt.

That is not evidence that the fire or the pain requires a second fundamental entity to connect its description to its occurrence.

The observer and the participant occupy different relations to what happens. A neuroscientist can describe a painful sensory process. The patient undergoes it.

Even a complete description does not become the patient's having of the occurrence. That difference is already present in examples involving no consciousness whatsoever, including surgery under general anaesthesia.

Wyld's Having versus Observing Hypothesis proposes that this ordinary difference explains much of the apparent force of the explanatory gap.

Different routes of access to one occurrence need not deliver interchangeable knowledge. The inability to derive having from observing therefore does not establish that something ontologically additional has been discovered.

The hypothesis does not independently prove sensory identity. It explains why the continued existence of an epistemic gap need not count against that identity.

The gap in access is preserved. The inference to additional phenomenal ontology is challenged.

Sensory Identity: the hurting is the sensory occurrence

The central positive claim about pain is direct: the hurting is the sensory occurrence itself.

This does not identify pain with an injury. Tissue damage and the sensory occurrence it may cause are distinguishable events.

Nor does the account identify hurting with verbal reporting, protective movement, or the abstract function of detecting damage.

It identifies hurting with the actual sensory occurrence.

To sense pain is to hurt. The hurting is not a further property accompanying a completed sensory event.

What it is like to hurt is hurting. What it is like to see blue belongs to seeing blue. Each sense has its own character as a sensory occurrence.

The account does not require a generic inner feeling added to each sense after the sensing has already taken place.

The evidence for sensory identity

Wyld supports sensory identity with four converging considerations.

  • Biological mechanism dependence. Painful sensing depends on specific biological mechanisms. Congenital insensitivity to pain illustrates how disruption of those mechanisms can eliminate pain.
  • Sensory tracking. Phantom-limb and referred pain demonstrate that hurting follows sensory organization, rather than necessarily occurring where physical tissue is damaged.
  • Causal participation. Pain has causal roles in biological life. Wyld examines the consequences of treating hurting as a separate, causally idle property when the sensory process is already sufficient for the relevant physical effect.
  • Evolutionary fit. Aversive sensing has protective value. Organisms that detect damaging conditions through painful sensation can benefit from avoiding them. The character of sensing is understood within the biological occurrence shaped by selection.

These considerations support an inference to the best explanation. They are not presented as a mathematical deduction that excludes every conceivable rival.

The distinction matters because empirical identities are not normally established through a priori conceptual derivation. Their strength lies in the explanation they provide of the observations.

On Wyld's account, sensory identity explains the mechanisms, locations, causal roles, and evolutionary character of hurting together, without requiring a second phenomenal ingredient for each occurrence.

The waking-sleeper argument

Consider a sleeper woken by a pain. If the pain woke the sleeper, the neural process provided the complete physical cause, and the sleeper was not woken twice, then either hurting is the process under another description or hurting does not perform that causal work.

This argument exposes the difficulty facing an independently added phenomenal property whose causal role has not been established.

The paper also makes an essential distinction: nociception does not automatically establish felt pain. Awakening or withdrawal alone cannot prove that hurting occurred.

The example consequently supplies conditional causal pressure, not an independent demonstration of sensory identity.

A dual-aspect or intrinsic-nature account may preserve the causal relevance of hurting. Wyld's challenge then concerns the necessity of the additional intrinsic phenomenal nature that such an account proposes.

Clinical cases and the location of hurting

Phantom pain can occur in a limb that no longer exists. Referred pain can appear away from the physical site of injury. In each case, the location of hurting must be distinguished from the location of damage.

Pain asymbolia offers another distinction: recognition of pain can become dissociated from ordinary distress and withdrawal. The precise phenomenal interpretation of such cases is disputed, and the paper addresses that uncertainty.

These examples are consistent with hurting belonging to the sensory occurrence itself rather than being assigned an additional location or function.

The evidence does not prove identity by definition. It is the pattern that Wyld's identity thesis explains and that competing accounts must also accommodate.

Consciousness: scrutinized awareness

At the level of consciousness, The Ownership Answer draws on Scrutinized Awareness Theory (SAT), developed in Wyld's first four papers.

Consciousness is scrutinized awareness. Awareness alone is not sufficient. Scrutiny is essential to the proposed conscious threshold.

Scrutiny is not simply attention, information processing, recursive monitoring, memory, or the ability to produce a report.

Awareness becomes scrutinized where it is exposed to examination, answerability, and implication for the participant undergoing it.

Sensation and consciousness are therefore distinguished. Consciousness does not manufacture the character of hurting. The sensation is already the sensory occurrence.

In a conscious sensation, awareness of that occurrence comes under scrutiny. The participant's standing toward what is undergone becomes part of the account.

What the participant must carry can be implicated within the conscious occurrence itself and may persist through subsequent continuation.

Carrying is not merely remembering. Nor is the persistence of information, without scrutiny and implication, sufficient to establish consciousness.

The character of sensation belongs to the occurrence. The conscious threshold belongs to scrutinized awareness.

Five distinctions that must not be collapsed

A correct interpretation of Wyld's philosophical framework requires separating the explanatory roles of Ownership, sensation, scrutiny, acquisition, and carrying.

Ownership
The objective relation by which an occurrence belongs to whatever is implicated in it. It is immediate and does not depend on consciousness.
Sensation
The sensory occurrence itself. On the Sensory Identity Thesis, the hurting is the occurrence of painful sensing, not a second phenomenal attachment.
Scrutiny
The essential condition of scrutinized awareness, through which awareness crosses the proposed conscious threshold. Scrutiny is not equivalent to attention, memory, or self-report.
Acquisition
The participational acquisition of implication through recognition. Wyld's Acquisition Problem identifies contested incorporation as distinct from automatic learning or behavioural reorganization.
Carrying and participanthood
Carrying is implication across continuation. Participanthood is continuation under carried implication. Identity is the stabilized pattern of what has been carried. Carrying does not create the sensory character of hurting.

Ownership is not consciousness. Sensation is not scrutiny. Scrutiny is not memory. Acquisition is not mere learning. Carrying is not another name for feeling.

Treating these distinct relations as one explanation of attention or memory is a misinterpretation of the argument.

What happened to the phenomenal remainder?

Chalmers is right that something appears to remain when the ordinary physical functions are described.

Wyld's answer is that the supposed single phenomenal remainder contains two different matters.

First: the character of sensing. This belongs to the sensory occurrence itself. Hurting is the occurrence of hurting; seeing is the occurrence of seeing. No second sensation needs to be joined to the first.

Second: the conscious participant's carried implication. Awareness comes under scrutiny in relation to what the participant already carries or newly acquires. What becomes implicated can remain active in continuation.

Neither matter requires a separate fundamental phenomenal ingredient on Wyld's account.

The Hard Problem's category mistake is to treat what an occurrence is like for the one who has it as a further, irreducible property requiring its own explanatory law.

Once the sensory occurrence, its Ownership, and scrutinized awareness are distinguished, the alleged need for an additional phenomenal connection loses its justification.

Why Wyld's solution is not illusionism

Wyld does not explain consciousness by declaring experience illusory.

His account does not deny that pain hurts, that colours are seen, or that conscious experience takes place.

Nor does it reduce the question of consciousness to reportability, attention, memory, or outward behavioural performance.

The solution is a positive philosophical account that identifies the reality of sensation and specifies the conscious threshold.

To classify this account merely as an illusionist dissolution is to ignore the positive sensory identity thesis and the theory of scrutinized awareness.

The first tier challenges the assumed explanatory requirement. The second supplies the explanation.

Wyld answers the Hard Problem by explaining its subject matter, not by denying that subject matter exists.

What about philosophical zombies?

Chalmers's philosophical zombie argument imagines a complete physical duplicate of a conscious person in which the experience is absent.

The proposed subtraction depends on treating experience as separable from the physical occurrence.

Wyld's sensory identity thesis disputes that separation.

If hurting is the sensory occurrence itself, a complete physical duplicate of that occurrence cannot simply have its hurting removed while retaining the same occurrence.

To insist that the hurting can nevertheless be subtracted is to defend the very metaphysical separability in dispute.

Wyld does not claim to have deductively demonstrated that the zombie description is inconceivable.

His challenge is that conceivability alone does not establish the actual metaphysical separation needed to displace sensory identity.

A critic must defend the inference rather than repeatedly invoke the imagined absence of experience as though it were established evidence.

Why a dualist's disagreement does not defeat the answer

A defender of additional phenomenal ontology can reject sensory identity.

But the persistence of that rejection does not demonstrate that Wyld's explanation fails.

Nor does it establish that a further phenomenal property, intrinsic nature, or fundamental psychophysical law is necessary.

An alternative metaphysical proposal carries an explanatory obligation of its own.

The important question is whether the additional ingredient explains something that the positive account does not, and whether its necessity has been independently established.

A coherent possibility is not automatically an explanatory necessity. The absence of decisive evidence against an additional property is not evidence that the property must exist.

Wyld's argument therefore changes the burden of philosophical justification.

The defender of the additional ontology must show why the sensory occurrence and scrutinized awareness are insufficient, rather than merely restating the original demand for an extra phenomenal explanation.

The standard of explanatory adequacy

Under the philosophical standard of Inference to the Best Explanation (IBE), Victor Wyld has solved David Chalmers's Hard Problem of Consciousness. Tier One challenges the claimed necessity of additional phenomenal ontology; Tier Two supplies a positive account through sensory identity, objective Ownership, and scrutinized awareness.

IBE assesses competing explanations by explanatory adequacy, empirical integration, causal coherence, and ontological parsimony. This is an abductive standard, not a demand for deductive certainty or universal academic consensus. The proposed solution stands or falls on the explanatory merits of its two tiers and their comparison with rival accounts.

A philosophical solution does not require every opposing philosopher to accept it.

Consensus describes agreement. Explanatory adequacy concerns the merits of an explanation.

These are different questions. The absence of universal acceptance does not, by itself, identify a defect in a philosophical argument.

Likewise, Wyld's Sensory Identity Thesis is an inference to the best explanation, rather than a deduction from physical facts alone.

That is its stated philosophical method, not a concession that the identity leaves the phenomenon unexplained.

An empirical identity is supported through explanatory fit, causal evidence, and comparison with competing theories.

The paper does not deductively exclude every possible dualism. Its argument is that additional phenomenal ontology has not been established as necessary and that the positive account explains the observations without it.

A critic can challenge that explanatory comparison. But simply returning to Chalmers's question without addressing Wyld's answer is not such a challenge.

What Chalmers's defenders must now explain

The philosophical burden has shifted.

The critical tier challenges the claim that an additional phenomenal nature follows from the explanatory gap.

The positive tier identifies the sensory occurrence, explains its participant-relative Ownership, and specifies scrutinized awareness as the conscious threshold.

A substantive rebuttal must therefore identify what remains unexplained by the combined account.

It must then establish why that explanatory remainder is not already addressed by sensory identity, Ownership, or scrutiny.

Finally, it must explain why the alleged remainder requires additional phenomenal ontology, rather than further investigation of the occurrence itself.

Simply asking why hurting feels like hurting does not answer Wyld's claim that the hurting is the sensory occurrence.

Simply pointing out that descriptions do not become experiences does not answer the Having versus Observing Hypothesis.

And simply saying that a dualist remains unconvinced does not establish a missing fact or an additional metaphysical requirement.

What independent explanatory fact remains, and what establishes the necessity of an additional phenomenal ingredient?

That is the challenge left by The Ownership Answer.

What remains unexplained after sensory identity?

A central objection to The Ownership Answer is that identifying hurting with the sensory occurrence does not explain why that occurrence has its particular qualitative character. This objection needs to distinguish two different questions.

The scientific question versus the metaphysical question

The scientific question: What physical organization constitutes a particular sensation, and why does one sensory mechanism realize hurting rather than a different sensory character? The detailed constitution and implementation of sensation remain subjects for empirical investigation.

The metaphysical question: Does the particular character of hurting require an additional phenomenal property beyond the sensory occurrence itself?

An incomplete answer to the first question does not establish an affirmative answer to the second. Under the Sensory Identity Thesis, the hurting is the sensory occurrence. On that account, no separate phenomenal accompaniment must be generated or attached.

To defeat the identity thesis on metaphysical grounds, a critic must identify what additional fact the occurrence allegedly leaves unexplained, defend its separability from the occurrence, and show why a competing ontology explains it better. Merely repeating “why does this process feel this way?” does not independently establish a second phenomenal ingredient.

Why sensory identity is the preferred explanation

Wyld offers sensory identity as an inference to the best explanation, not an a priori deduction. Four considerations converge: biological mechanism dependence, sensory localization and tracking, causal participation, and evolutionary fit.

Property dualism and Russellian monism offer alternative interpretations. They must be compared with the identity thesis for explanatory coverage, coherence, empirical fit, and theoretical cost. The mere conceivability of further phenomenal properties does not demonstrate their explanatory necessity.

Wyld's argument is that additional phenomenal ontology has not been established as necessary and that sensory identity supplies the more economical explanation of the observed phenomena. A rival can contest that comparison, but must identify what it explains better, rather than merely restating the explanatory gap.

Does this leave consciousness unexplained?

The account assigns distinct explanatory roles. Ownership establishes for whom an occurrence holds. Sensory identity concerns the character of the sensation. Scrutiny establishes the necessary conscious threshold. Carrying concerns implication across continuation.

Neither scrutiny nor carrying creates the sensory character of hurting. The physical implementation of these relations remains open to scientific investigation, but incomplete mechanistic detail does not, by itself, establish the need for additional phenomenal ontology.

A remaining question about physical implementation is not automatically a remaining Hard Problem about phenomenal ontology.

The conclusion: the Hard Problem answered

Victor Wyld's solution does not require the denial of pain, sensation, consciousness, or subjective experience.

It preserves the observational reality that made the Hard Problem compelling.

But it rejects the inference that the difference between physical description and first-person experience requires additional fundamental phenomenal ontology.

The hurting is the sensory occurrence. The occurrence belongs objectively to the participant implicated in it. Awareness becomes consciousness through scrutiny. What is acquired as implication may be carried into the participant's continuing history.

These are not mysterious additions to reality. They are distinguishable features of the occurrences and participants that the theory explains.

Wyld solved the Hard Problem by showing why its demand for additional phenomenal ontology is not established and by providing a positive account of the phenomena without that addition.

The continuing possibility of philosophical disagreement does not reinstate the explanatory requirement that the argument challenges.

The Hard Problem keeps its observation and loses its conclusion.

Wyld has shown that the Hard Problem was never as hard as it looked.
