The Ownership Answer

What It Is Like

Published October 24, 2026

Publication record: Zenodo · PhilPapers · DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.23617773

The Ownership Answer: What It Is Like is the fifth paper in Victor Wyld's 2026 philosophical sequence on consciousness, participation, and subjective experience. It addresses David Chalmers's Hard Problem of consciousness, arguing that the problem's observations do not establish the additional phenomenal ontology that Chalmers concludes is required.

The paper advances a two-tier argument. The first examines the premises and explanatory direction underlying the Hard Problem. The second develops a positive account of sensation and consciousness through objective Ownership, sensory identity, scrutiny, and the carrying of implication.

Its central answer is Ownership: the objective relation by which an occurrence belongs to whatever is implicated in it. The process and the experience are proposed to be one occurrence, belonging to its implicated participant rather than requiring a further phenomenal ingredient to connect them.

The answer proposed here is one word: Ownership.

The Hard Problem and its assumption

In his 1995 paper Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness, David Chalmers asks why the performance of physical functions is accompanied by experience.

Wyld begins by questioning the explanatory direction embedded in that formulation. The question treats physical processing as the starting point and experience as something further, something that must be connected to the physical account.

But what is actually observed is that sensation and physical processes occur together. That they accompany one another does not establish that either is an independently constituted addition to the other.

The paper examines Chalmers's original wording, distinguishing his observations of regularity, correspondence, and coherence from his repeated descriptions of experience as arising, emerging, or being produced by physical processes.

The difference matters. A correlation does not establish the direction of explanation, and a failure to derive one description from another does not, by itself, establish the existence of a second phenomenal reality.

The reversal

Wyld reverses the familiar formulation of the Hard Problem to expose the assumption beneath it.

Why is experience accompanied by the performance of these functions? Who says that it is?

The reversal does not deny either experience or physical processes. It asks what entitles the inquiry to treat the physical as the explanatory base and experience as an unexpected addition.

For the person undergoing pain, the hurting is immediately given. The neural mechanisms responsible for its constitution are learned about later through observation, scientific investigation, and report.

The direction of discovery is therefore different from the direction assumed by the Hard Problem. Neither direction alone establishes the metaphysical priority of one description over the other.

Wyld also reverses the problem of conceptual derivation. A complete description of physical mechanisms may not allow someone to derive what an occurrence is like for its participant. Equally, knowing a sensation from having it does not allow someone to derive the complete physical mechanisms underlying that sensation.

The resulting gap runs in both directions. Its existence does not establish which side requires an additional ontological ingredient.

Ownership: the objective relation

Ownership is the immediate and lasting relation by which an occurrence belongs to whatever is objectively implicated in it.

Ownership does not require consciousness, awareness, recognition, attention, or memory. It begins at the moment of implication.

A patient undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia owns the occurrence of an amputation without being conscious of it. The loss is already theirs before they awaken, recognize what happened, or begin to carry its consequences consciously.

The surgeon also owns the occurrence, but differently: as the person who performs the procedure rather than as the person whose limb is removed. The same occurrence can implicate more than one participant in different positions.

Ownership therefore does not mean ordinary possession. It describes a relation between an occurrence and what is implicated in that occurrence.

Nor does Ownership alone establish consciousness. The occurrence belongs to its participant whether or not the participant is conscious of what has happened.

Ownership is immediate. Acquisition is an event. Carrying is continuation.

The difference between having and observing

The distinction between having an occurrence and observing or describing it is central to Wyld's positive account.

A surgeon can observe and perform an operation. The patient undergoes it. Each is implicated in the same occurrence, but neither occupies the same position within it.

Likewise, a neuroscientist may describe the mechanisms of pain from observation, while the person experiencing that pain knows the occurrence from having it.

These are not necessarily two different facts. They may instead be two non-interchangeable ways of knowing one occurrence.

Wyld proposes that this distinction helps explain the persistence of the explanatory gap. Knowing something through observation does not reproduce the participant's having of it. But that epistemic difference does not require an additional phenomenal property.

The having-and-observing account is offered as an explanatory hypothesis. Its adequacy is part of the paper's positive thesis, rather than a result established independently of it.

Sensation: what it is like to hurt

The paper distinguishes the physical event that produces a sensation from the sensory occurrence itself.

Damage to the body may generate a painful sensory response. The damage and the hurting are not the same event considered without distinction.

Wyld's positive identity thesis concerns the sensation: the hurting is the sensory occurrence itself.

Hurting does not require an additional feeling attached to an otherwise complete sensory occurrence. To sense pain is to hurt. The hurting is not something further accompanying the sensing.

The paper develops this claim through several converging observations:

  • Mechanism dependence — the occurrence of pain depends upon particular biological mechanisms, including mechanisms disrupted in congenital insensitivity to pain.
  • Sensory location — phantom and referred pain demonstrate that hurting follows the sensory occurrence, rather than necessarily corresponding to the location of physical damage.
  • Causal relevance — pain participates in waking, withdrawal, avoidance, and protective responses. The paper examines the causal role of hurting rather than accepting its exclusion as an independently established fact.
  • Evolutionary fit — aversive sensation can protect organisms from injury, giving natural selection reason to favour the character of sensing observed in biological life.

These observations support the proposed identity as an inference to the best explanation. They do not deductively prove it or establish that every rival account is logically impossible.

The evolutionary argument explains why aversive sensing would be favoured. It does not, by itself, refute the philosophical possibility of a zombie with identical behaviour but no experience.

The positive claim remains that the sensory occurrence itself accounts for the character Chalmers treats as a further phenomenal property.

Consciousness: scrutiny and carrying

At the level of consciousness, The Ownership Answer draws upon Wyld's Scrutinized Awareness Theory (SAT).

SAT proposes that awareness becomes consciousness when it becomes something the participant must carry.

Awareness and consciousness are therefore not identical. A person may register, sense, or respond to something without that awareness necessarily having crossed the conscious threshold.

The threshold involves scrutiny: awareness exposed to implication for the participant who owns the occurrence.

What must be carried is already part of the conscious experience as scrutiny takes place. Carrying need not wait until that experience has ended.

Nor is carrying the same as remembering. An implication may continue in the participant's life without remaining the subject of active thought or conscious recollection.

Consciousness thus adds something to an occurrence, but not an additional phenomenal substance or unexplained inner feel. What it adds is awareness under scrutiny, together with what the participant must carry.

What remains of the Hard Problem?

Wyld accepts the observation that something remains when physical functions have been described.

The paper disputes Chalmers's conclusion about what that remainder must be.

On Wyld's account, two different matters have been treated as one.

  • The character of sensation. This belongs to the sensory occurrence itself. The hurting is the hurting; the seen blue belongs to seeing.
  • The carried remainder of consciousness. This concerns awareness under scrutiny and what becomes something the participant must carry.

Neither requires a separate phenomenal ingredient on the explanation advanced in the paper.

The philosophical error Wyld identifies is one of category: treating what an occurrence is like for the one who has it as an additional, irreducible property of the occurrence requiring its own law.

Ownership explains whose occurrence it is. Sensory identity addresses the character of the sensing. Scrutinized awareness addresses the conscious participant's carrying.

These are distinct explanatory relations. Their separation is central to the paper's answer.

Two tiers of argument

The paper distinguishes its critical case from its positive explanation.

First tier: the critical audit. Chalmers's evidence establishes relationships between physical processes and experience. His arguments for conceptual separability do not, without further premises, establish that additional phenomenal ontology is required.

This audit includes the direction of his explanatory question, the reversibility of conceptual non-entailment, and the distinction between epistemic separability and metaphysical separation.

Second tier: the positive account. Ownership, sensory identity, and scrutinized awareness provide an explanation of subjective experience without additional fundamental phenomenal properties or psychophysical laws.

The two tiers are related but not logically identical. A critic may reject the positive identity thesis without thereby establishing that Chalmers has demonstrated the necessity of further phenomenal ontology.

What the argument establishes

The Ownership Answer challenges both the original 1995 formulation of the Hard Problem and the developed arguments that draw upon ideal conceivability, metaphysical possibility, and the intrinsic nature of physical things.

Wyld contests the transition from the conceivability of a physical duplicate without experience to the metaphysical possibility of such a duplicate.

He also challenges the necessity of a further phenomenal or protophenomenal intrinsic nature beneath the structural description of physical reality.

The paper does not establish that every anti-physicalist theory is false. Nor does it claim to have deductively proved that hurting is identical with its sensory occurrence.

It argues that Chalmers has not established the additional ontology his conclusion requires and that Wyld's positive account explains the relevant observations without that addition.

The remaining philosophical dispute concerns whether an additional intrinsic phenomenal nature is nevertheless needed, and whether the identity thesis offers the best explanation of what is observed.

The paper leaves those claims open to philosophical criticism and further empirical investigation, particularly into the constitution and causal role of sensory pain.

The five-paper philosophical sequence

The Ownership Answer is the culmination of four foundational papers published earlier in 2026.

  1. On the Question of Scrutinized Awareness (May 22, 2026) introduces SAT and the proposed conscious threshold.
  2. The Recompression Problem (May 29, 2026) examines how distinctions collapse under conceptual and institutional recompression.
  3. The Participanthood Problem: Identity Under Continued Carrying (July 1, 2026) develops participanthood and identity under continued implication.
  4. The Acquisition Problem: Participational Recognition and the Structural Acquisition of Implication (August 15, 2026) examines acquired implication through participational recognition.
  5. The Ownership Answer: What It Is Like (October 24, 2026) applies the developed framework to the Hard Problem of consciousness.

Together, the five papers address the conscious threshold, the preservation of philosophical distinctions, participanthood, the acquisition of implication, and the relation between sensory occurrences and subjective experience.

The conclusion

The Hard Problem begins with something real: conscious beings see, hear, sense, suffer, and undergo occurrences that belong to them.

What Wyld disputes is the conclusion that these observations establish a further fundamental ingredient of reality.

An occurrence belongs to the participant implicated in it. The sensory occurrence has its own character. When awareness comes under scrutiny, consciousness concerns what the participant must carry.

On this account, the explanatory gap does not disappear because experience is denied. Its ontological significance is reconsidered because the distinction between having and observing need not describe two different things.

Victor Wyld has shown how The Hard Problem, while keeping its observation, loses its conclusion.
