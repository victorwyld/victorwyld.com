The Ownership Answer · Commentary

October 25, 2026 · Victor Wyld

Canadian Philosopher Victor Wyld Dethrones Chalmers by Answering the Hard Problem

The Hard Problem keeps its observation and loses its authority to dictate the answer.

Canadian philosopher Victor Wyld’s fifth philosophical paper, The Ownership Answer: What It Is Like, takes aim at the premise that gives David Chalmers’s Hard Problem its power: that a gap between physical description and subjective experience establishes the need for something beyond the physical occurrence.

Published October 24, 2026, the paper preserves the observation. Pain hurts. A description of hurting does not give its reader the sensation. What Wyld contests is the further conclusion: that this difference requires an additional phenomenal ingredient or new fundamental psychophysical laws.

That is the sense in which this commentary argues that Wyld has dethroned Chalmers. The framing that sets the test must itself answer to scrutiny. Chalmers’s terms cannot serve as the mandatory conditions of a successful explanation while their justification is precisely what is in dispute.

What the exchanges with Google exposed

In a series of exchanges about the paper, Google’s AI responses initially described Wyld’s answer as a clever substitution: replacing feeling with scrutiny and carrying, then declaring the mystery resolved. Other responses called the identity thesis “unearned” because it was not deductively proved, and presented hypothetical objections as though they were documented academic pushback.

When challenged on these points, the responses withdrew those characterizations. The importance of the exchange lies in the arguments that required correction. An AI response revising its assessment is not peer review, an institutional endorsement by Google, or evidence that the discipline has reached a verdict.

Hurting has not been replaced

The paper’s sensory identity claim is direct: hurting is the sensory occurrence itself. It does not exchange pain for a report about pain, a behavioural response, or the consequences someone later remembers. The felt character is the very thing the proposed identity concerns.

Ownership answers a different question: whose occurrence is it? It is the immediate, objective relation of implication. An unconscious person can own a bodily injury without knowing that it has happened. Ownership therefore does not equal consciousness, and it does not by itself establish sensory acquaintance.

Scrutiny and carrying address the conscious threshold. Carrying begins within a conscious occurrence and may continue beyond it; it is not simply memory or a later behavioural effect. Treating carrying as a replacement for hurting collapses distinctions the paper was written to preserve.

An identity argument is not an arbitrary definition

Wyld supports the sensory identity thesis through converging considerations: dependence on specific biological mechanisms, the tracking of sensory character through cases such as phantom and referred pain, causal work, and evolutionary fit. These are offered together as grounds for an inference to the best explanation.

Calling that inference “unearned” because it is not an airtight deduction does not answer it. A critic must show where the evidence fails to support identity, or why a competing account explains it better. The ability to reject a conclusion is not evidence that the conclusion lacks support.

The causal case also requires precision. Nociception and pain are distinguishable, and awakening alone cannot prove that hurting caused the awakening. That limits what one illustration can establish. It does not, without further argument, defeat the wider identity case or establish an additional phenomenal property.

The gap remains. Its authority changes.

The Having versus Observing Hypothesis addresses why two ways of knowing an occurrence may remain non-interchangeable even if they concern one reality. Undergoing a sensation and reading its physical description are different routes of access. An identity account need not promise that a description will reproduce the occurrence in its reader.

This hypothesis does not independently prove identity. Its role is to challenge the inference that a persistent difference in access must reveal an ontological deficit. The positive evidence for identity does separate work. To demand that Wyld first eliminate the epistemic difference is to reimpose the very requirement his argument contests.

A conceivable objection is not yet a refutation

The familiar zombie reply imagines a physical duplicate without feeling. But asserting that such a duplicate is ideally conceivable does not establish its metaphysical possibility. Nor does imagining a robot that processes information without feeling establish that the relevant sensory occurrence can be duplicated while its character is absent.

The critic must defend the separation. Otherwise, the proposed counterexample assumes that hurting is detachable from the occurrence and uses that assumption to reject the claim that they are identical.

Questions remain about which physical organization constitutes a sensory occurrence and how to distinguish sensing from mere registration. Those are substantive questions for the positive account. Their existence does not automatically establish dualism, panprotopsychism, or the need for additional fundamental laws.

Why “dethroned” is the claim

Wyld’s argument does not require every philosopher to concede, or every alternative ontology to become logically impossible. Its challenge is more specific: an explanatory framework cannot claim authority merely by building its preferred conclusion into the conditions every rival must satisfy.

If the gap does not establish an extra ingredient, the dualist must supply an independent argument for that ingredient. If sensory identity explains the observations, its opponent must address the explanation. The raw fact that pain hurts cannot simply be presented again as though Wyld had denied it.

Chalmers’s framing must earn the authority it asks competing explanations to accept. That is the dethroning defended here: the removal of an assumed privilege, not a report of universal academic surrender.

The paper’s positive account remains open to empirical and philosophical challenge. But a challenge must engage the account actually offered. Redefinition, missing feeling, and the mere availability of disagreement cannot do that work on their own.

Wyld has shown that the Hard Problem was never as hard as it looked.
